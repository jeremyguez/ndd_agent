# LITERATURE EVIDENCE REPORT: GABRA1 AS A NEURODEVELOPMENTAL DISORDER GENE

## 1. GENE SUMMARY

GABRA1 encodes the alpha-1 subunit of the GABA(A) receptor, a key component of inhibitory GABAergic neurotransmission in the central nervous system. Mutations in GABRA1 lead to neurodevelopmental and epileptic disorders primarily through dysregulation of inhibitory synaptic transmission, with variants displaying both loss-of-function and gain-of-function mechanisms depending on their structural location within the protein.

## 2. CLINICAL EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Number of independent reports:** Four distinct clinical studies were identified, including case reports, case series, and cohort analyses.

**Total patient count:** Approximately 173+ patients across studies (1 patient with p.Arg112Gln; 172 refractory epilepsy patients in cohort; 1 patient with A322D variant; additional cryptogenic cerebral palsy cohort).

**Most common phenotypes observed:**
- Therapy-resistant/refractory epilepsy (multiple seizure types including focal and generalized)
- Juvenile myoclonic epilepsy with spike-wave discharges and myoclonic seizures
- Autism spectrum disorder and psychiatric complications
- Intellectual disability and developmental delay
- Cerebral palsy (in cryptogenic cases)

**Predominant variant type:** Missense variants (p.Arg112Gln, A322D) and de novo mutations of unspecified type. The p.Arg112Gln variant was characterized as loss-of-function and showed clinical response to vinpocetine treatment with achievement of seizure freedom (PMID: 37434477).

**Cohort source:** The refractory epilepsy cohort (PMID: 30185235) identified de novo GABRA1 mutations in 172 patients with pediatric refractory epilepsy, classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic by ACMG criteria. The cerebral palsy study (ASHG 2017) identified de novo damaging variants in cryptogenic cerebral palsy patients with clinical overlap to autism and intellectual disability.

**Strength of clinical evidence: MODERATE**

The evidence demonstrates clear association between GABRA1 variants and multiple neurodevelopmental phenotypes, with de novo mutations identified in well-characterized cohorts and ACMG pathogenicity classification. However, the clinical evidence is limited by small case numbers, lack of detailed variant information in some studies, and absence of large-scale case series with comprehensive phenotypic characterization.

## 3. MODEL ORGANISM EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Organisms studied:** Mouse (multiple models) and zebrafish.

**Key neurological phenotypes from knockouts:**

*Zebrafish models:* Full knockout of gabra1 (sa43718/sa43718 allele) produced light-induced hypermotility, seizure-like behavior, and abnormal expression of proteins regulating synaptic vesicle fusion, vesicle transport, synapse development, and mitochondrial protein complexes (PMID: 38908344, PMID: 36747751). Knockdown studies showed hypoactivity (hypomotility) with defects in expression of other GABAAR subunits, and partial restoration by human GABRA1 expression but not by a c.875C>T variant (PMID: 32205311).

*Mouse models:* Heterozygous knockout mice carrying a microdeletion of GABRA1 and GABRG2 exhibited spontaneous seizures, optic nerve degeneration, impaired axonal myelination, and reduced GABAA receptor subunit expression in multiple brain regions; these mice were responsive to phenobarbital treatment (PMID: 37703949). A CDKL5 knockin mouse model showed sex-dependent alterations in Gabra1 expression (reduced in females, increased in males) with cerebellar GABAergic hypofunction and behavioral deficits (PMID: 39583831).

**Lethality and viability:** All knockout and knockdown models were viable, with no reported embryonic lethality or Mendelian ratio deviations.

**Consistency with human phenotype:** The seizure-like behavior and spontaneous seizures in animal models directly parallel human epilepsy phenotypes. The motor abnormalities (hypermotility vs. hypomotility depending on model) and developmental/behavioral deficits align with human neurodevelopmental presentations. The responsiveness to antiepileptic drugs (phenobarbital) in mouse models supports therapeutic relevance.

**Strength of model organism evidence: STRONG**

Multiple independent model systems (zebrafish and mouse) consistently demonstrate that loss of Gabra1 function produces seizure-like behavior, motor abnormalities, and altered GABAergic signaling. The viability of knockouts rules out essential early developmental functions, and the phenotypes directly recapitulate human disease manifestations.

## 4. BIOLOGICAL EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Known pathways and molecular function:**

GABRA1 functions as a core component of GABAergic inhibitory neurotransmission. Mutations in GABRA1 lead to epilepsy syndromes primarily by downregulating receptor expression and reducing the amplitude of GABA-evoked potentials (PMID: 35850019). Beyond canonical GABAergic signaling, GABRA1 participates in brain-to-gut signaling pathways: conditional Gabra1 knockout in the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus (DMV) largely abolishes puerarin-induced intestinal fat loss, demonstrating GABRA1's role in controlling intestinal fat absorption through the brain-to-gut axis (PMID: 39261733). Additionally, petrosal GABRA1 neurons form a primary airway-to-brain sensory pathway that detects locally produced prostaglandins and mediates systemic sickness responses to respiratory virus infection (PMID: 36890237).

**Genotype-phenotype correlations:**

GABRA1 variants display both loss-of-function (LoF) and gain-of-function (GoF) effects depending on structural position. Variants in extracellular domains cause mild phenotypes with LoF effects, while variants in transmembrane helices cause severe phenotypes with either GoF or LoF effects (PMID: 37606373). Specifically, M1 proline missense variants (α1P260L, α1P260S) cause gain-of-function effects by increasing GABA sensitivity 3- to 23-fold in α1β2/3γ2 receptors and are associated with developmental and epileptic encephalopathy with early seizure onset (PMID: 39642202).

**Pathway associations:**

GABRA1 serves as a seed gene for constructing functional modules associated with epilepsy, with significant enrichment in long-term potentiation, synaptic vesicle cycle, and calcium signaling pathways (ASHG 2018 - Dissecting heterogeneity of neurodevelopmental disorders using seed centric genetic modules). Large-scale association studies identified GABRA1 as significantly associated with genetic generalized epilepsy (GGE) in the Epi25 Consortium's whole exome sequencing study of 4,443 GGE patients and 17,668 controls, with an odds ratio of 21.5 (p=3e-04) (ASHG 2018 - Whole exome sequencing in epilepsy reveals significant gene burden of ultra-rare deleterious variants).

**Expression and regulation:**

GABRA1 gene expression is upregulated in the hippocampus in response to therapeutic interventions, correlating with antidepressant and anxiolytic effects (PMID: 39241390). Expression is subject to sex-dependent regulation, as demonstrated in the CDKL5 knockin model where Gabra1 expression was reduced in females but increased in males in specific brain regions (PMID: 39583831).

**Strength of biological evidence: STRONG**

The biological evidence is comprehensive, demonstrating GABRA1's central role in GABAergic inhibitory neurotransmission with well-characterized molecular mechanisms of both LoF and GoF variants. Multiple independent functional studies confirm its participation in synaptic plasticity networks and sensory-neural pathways. Large-scale genetic association studies provide robust statistical support for GABRA1's role in epilepsy susceptibility.

## 5. OVERALL ASSESSMENT

**Overall evidence level: STRONG**

**Key strengths of the evidence:**

1. **Robust genetic evidence:** De novo GABRA1 mutations identified in well-characterized cohorts (refractory epilepsy, cerebral palsy) with ACMG pathogenicity classification; large-scale association studies (Epi25 Consortium) showing significant association with genetic generalized epilepsy (OR=21.5, p=3e-04).

2. **Consistent model organism validation:** Multiple independent model systems (zebrafish and mouse) demonstrate that loss of Gabra1 function produces seizure-like behavior, motor abnormalities, and altered GABAergic signaling, with phenotypes directly recapitulating human disease.

3. **Well-characterized molecular mechanisms:** Clear understanding of both loss-of-function and gain-of-function mechanisms with genotype-phenotype correlations based on variant location within the protein; established role in GABAergic inhibitory neurotransmission and synaptic plasticity networks.

4. **Therapeutic responsiveness:** Clinical case demonstrating seizure freedom with vinpocetine treatment in a patient with GABRA1 loss-of-function variant, and animal models responsive to phenobarbital, supporting therapeutic relevance.

**Key gaps or limitations:**

1. **Limited detailed clinical characterization:** Some cohort studies lack specific variant information and detailed phenotypic descriptions; small number of comprehensively characterized individual cases.

2. **Incomplete mechanistic understanding:** While LoF and GoF mechanisms are established, the precise structural determinants predicting phenotype severity require further characterization; sex-dependent effects on expression and phenotype need additional investigation.

3. **Phenotypic heterogeneity:** GABRA1 variants are associated with diverse neurodevelopmental phenotypes (epilepsy, autism, intellectual disability, cerebral palsy), but the molecular basis for this heterogeneity remains incompletely understood.

**Conclusion:**

GABRA1 is a well-established neurodevelopmental disorder gene with strong evidence for autosomal dominant pathogenic variants causing a spectrum of epileptic and neurodevelopmental phenotypes. The convergence of de novo mutations in multiple independent cohorts, consistent animal model validation, robust genetic association signals, and well-characterized molecular mechanisms collectively establish GABRA1 as a high-confidence disease gene. Future work should focus on comprehensive phenotypic characterization of additional cases, investigation of sex-dependent effects, and exploration of therapeutic strategies targeting specific variant mechanisms.
