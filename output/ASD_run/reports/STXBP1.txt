# LITERATURE EVIDENCE REPORT: STXBP1 AS A NEURODEVELOPMENTAL DISORDER GENE

## 1. GENE SUMMARY

STXBP1 encodes Munc18-1, a syntaxin-binding protein essential for synaptic vesicle fusion and neurotransmitter release at the presynaptic terminal. Pathogenic variants in STXBP1 result in haploinsufficiency and impaired neurotransmitter release, leading to dysfunction in neuronal communication and neurodevelopmental disease (PMID: 38137001).

## 2. CLINICAL EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Number of Studies and Patient Population:**
Nine independent clinical studies were identified, encompassing approximately 185 patients with confirmed or suspected STXBP1 pathogenic variants. The largest systematic review included 147 patients with 33 novel STXBP1 mutations (PMID: 26865513), while a natural history study followed 30 patients into adulthood (PMID: 35851549). Additional evidence comes from a Chinese autism cohort (PMID: 27824329), an Indian infantile epileptic spasms cohort (PMID: 37583270), and several case reports and conference abstracts.

**Most Common Phenotypes:**
The clinical presentation is remarkably consistent across studies:
- **Epilepsy**: Present in 95-100% of patients, with median seizure onset at 3.5 months (PMID: 35851549)
- **Intellectual disability**: Severe to profound in 87-88% of patients (PMID: 26865513, PMID: 35851549)
- **Developmental delay and regression**: Observed in 59% of patients, with 100% showing developmental impairment (PMID: 35851549)
- **Seizure types**: Infantile spasms (21-86% depending on cohort), tonic seizures, focal seizures, and tonic-clonic seizures
- **Treatment-resistant seizures**: Present in 80% of patients (PMID: 35851549)
- **Movement disorders**: Gait disorders (100% of ambulatory patients), tremor (56%, predominantly intention/action type), stereotypies (63%), and postural abnormalities (PMID: 35851549)
- **Autism spectrum disorder and autistic features**: Present in 42% of the natural history cohort (PMID: 35851549)
- **Behavioral problems**: Documented in 65% of patients (PMID: 35851549)
- **Nonverbal status**: 71% of patients are nonverbal (PMID: 35851549)
- **Functional dependence**: 100% of patients require assistance with activities of daily living (PMID: 35851549)

Specific epilepsy syndromes include Ohtahara syndrome (21%), West syndrome (9.5%), and Early Infantile Epileptic Encephalopathy type 4 (EIEE4) (PMID: 26865513, PMID: 31221716).

**Predominant Variant Type:**
The majority of pathogenic variants are de novo, consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance. Variant types include loss-of-function mutations (frameshift deletions, nonsense mutations), large deletions (ranging from 50 kb to 2 Mb), intragenic deletions, and missense variants. A natural history study of 162 individuals revealed genotype-phenotype correlations: protein-truncating variants and deletions showed infantile spasms with seizure remission, while missense variants showed focal seizures and ongoing seizures (PMID: 38015929).

**Cohort Membership:**
The studies do not explicitly indicate enrollment in major international cohorts (UDN, GREGoR, DDD, RUMC, GEL), though the evidence comes from international networks of clinicians and geneticists, medical genetics departments, and epilepsy centers across multiple countries (USA, Canada, China, India, and international collaborations).

**Strength of Clinical Evidence: STRONG**

The clinical evidence is strong, supported by: (1) large case series with 147+ patients; (2) consistent phenotypic presentation across multiple independent cohorts; (3) de novo mutations in unaffected parents confirming pathogenicity; (4) natural history data extending into adulthood; (5) genotype-phenotype correlations; and (6) identification of STXBP1 as a significant contributor to infantile-onset epilepsy with a 21% diagnostic yield in specialized cohorts (PMID: ASHG 2013).

## 3. MODEL ORGANISM EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Organisms Studied:**
Multiple model organisms have been employed: mice (heterozygous and conditional knockouts, humanized knock-ins), zebrafish (full knockout), Drosophila melanogaster (heterozygous null), and C. elegans (humanized replacement model).

**Key Phenotypes from Knockouts:**

*Mouse Models (Heterozygous Knockout):*
- Seizures with myoclonic jerks and spike-wave discharges (PMID: 29538625)
- Cortical hyperexcitability and abnormal EEG activity (PMID: 29538625, PMID: 32073399, PMID: 38086378)
- Cognitive dysfunction and impaired cognitive performance (PMID: 29538625, PMID: 32073399)
- Psychiatric dysfunction and anxiety-like behavior (PMID: 29538625, PMID: 32073399)
- Motor dysfunction and developmental delay (PMID: 32073399)
- Reduced cortical inhibitory neurotransmission and impaired GABAergic synaptic transmission (PMID: 32073399)
- Absence seizures with spike-and-wave discharges (PMID: 31015467)
- Defective inhibition in feedforward microcircuits with failure to recruit inhibitory interneurons (PMID: 38086378)
- Impaired memory performance in reward-based learning and spatial memory (PMID: 38316990)
- Mendelian ratio: Viable heterozygotes with normal Mendelian inheritance
- Lethality: Viable (heterozygotes); humanized knock-in mice show perinatal lethality particularly in males (sub-Mendelian ratio) (PMID: 39229131)

*Zebrafish Model (Full Knockout):*
- Spontaneous electrographic seizures during larval stages (PMID: 35451230)
- Ictal-like events in midbrain responsive to clemizole and trazodone but not standard antiepileptic drugs (PMID: 35451230)
- Notably, seizures were resistant to phenytoin, valproate, esxicarbazepine, levetiracetam, and diazepam, but responsive to clemizole and trazodone (80-83% reduction in ictal-like events)

*Drosophila Model (Heterozygous Null of Rop, STXBP1 Homolog):*
- Exacerbated α-synuclein aggregation (PMID: ASHG 2023)
- Decreased dopamine neurons
- Reduced motor function in adults
- Compound eye degeneration
- Mendelian ratio: Heterozygotes viable; homozygotes embryonic lethal
- Lethality: Embryonic lethal (homozygous null)

**Consistency with Human Phenotype:**
The model organism evidence shows strong consistency with human disease. Seizures, cognitive dysfunction, motor dysfunction, and developmental delay observed in heterozygous mice directly parallel the human phenotype. The cortical hyperexcitability and impaired inhibitory neurotransmission identified in mice provide mechanistic insight into the seizure pathophysiology observed in patients. The zebrafish model's resistance to standard antiepileptic drugs mirrors the treatment-resistant seizures seen in 80% of human patients (PMID: 35851549). The Drosophila findings linking STXBP1 haploinsufficiency to α-synuclein pathology are particularly notable, as they suggest a potential connection between early-onset neurodevelopmental disease and later-life synucleinopathies (PMID: 31221716).

**Strength of Model Organism Evidence: STRONG**

The model organism evidence is strong, supported by: (1) multiple independent model systems (mouse, zebrafish, Drosophila, C. elegans) showing consistent neurological phenotypes; (2) viable heterozygotes with Mendelian inheritance in most models, consistent with autosomal dominant human disease; (3) seizures and cognitive dysfunction directly paralleling human phenotypes; (4) mechanistic studies identifying cortical hyperexcitability and impaired inhibitory neurotransmission; (5) pharmacological validation (seizure suppression by levetiracetam in mice, clemizole/trazodone in zebrafish); and (6) demonstration of functional rescue by wild-type STXBP1 in humanized models.

## 4. BIOLOGICAL EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Known Pathways and Molecular Function:**

STXBP1 encodes Munc18-1, a critical regulator of synaptic vesicle exocytosis. The protein functions as a syntaxin-binding protein that facilitates SNARE complex assembly and vesicle fusion at the presynaptic terminal. Pathogenic variants result in haploinsufficiency with reduced MUNC18-1 protein levels and impaired neurotransmitter release (PMID: 38137001, PMID: 38298782).

iPSC-derived neurons from six STXBP1-related disorder patients demonstrated reduced MUNC18-1 RNA and protein levels, dysregulation of synaptic proteins, and altered network activity patterns. Proteome-wide screening identified dysregulation of proteins related to synapse function and RNA processes, with patient clustering suggesting distinctive pathobiology (PMID: 38298782).

**Expression Pattern:**

STXBP1 is highly expressed in the brain and is essential for neuronal development and function. The gene's critical role in synaptic transmission makes it particularly relevant to neurodevelopmental disorders characterized by seizures and cognitive dysfunction.

**Key Experimental Findings:**

1. **Synaptic Dysfunction Mechanisms**: Multiple studies have identified distinct mechanisms of synaptic dysfunction:
   - Reduced cortical inhibitory neurotransmission with distinct deficits in parvalbumin-expressing and somatostatin-expressing interneurons (PMID: 32073399)
   - Impaired cortico-striatal excitatory transmission triggering absence seizures (PMID: 31015467)
   - Defective inhibition in feedforward microcircuits with failure of excitatory synapses to recruit inhibitory interneurons, largely sparing parvalbumin-positive interneuron synapses (PMID: 38086378)
   - CX516 ampakine treatment restored interneuron recruitment and prevented hyperexcitability in mouse models (PMID: 38086378)

2. **Doc2 Protein Interaction**: Disease-linked mutations in Munc18-1 deplete synaptic Doc2A/B levels, impairing Doc2A/B synaptic targeting. Doc2A/B overexpression partially rescued synaptic dysfunction in heterozygous knockout neurons but not in neurons expressing the disease-causing G544D Munc18-1 mutant (PMID: 38242640).

3. **Lipid Metabolism and Memory**: STXBP1 controls targeting of DDHD2 to the plasma membrane and generation of saturated free fatty acids in the brain, linking STXBP1 to long-term memory via myristic acid (C14:0) generation during memory acquisition (PMID: 38316990).

4. **α-Synuclein Interaction**: STXBP1 (Munc18-1) functions as a molecular chaperone for α-synuclein, controlling its self-replicating aggregation. This finding links early-onset neurodevelopmental disorders caused by STXBP1 mutations to synucleinopathies (PMID: 31221716). In Drosophila, haploinsufficiency of Rop (STXBP1 homolog) exacerbated α-synuclein aggregation and pathology, with trehalose feeding reversing these phenotypes (PMID: ASHG 2023).

5. **Non-Synaptic Functions**: Conditional knockout studies revealed cell-autonomous cell death in sparse Stxbp1 knockout neurons and impaired dendritic growth, with altered localization of alpha II Spectrin and ARPC2, suggesting disrupted trafficking of membrane cytoskeleton proteins (PMID: 41510908).

6. **Viral Interaction**: HSV-1 latency-associated transcript microRNAs (miR-H3 and miR-H4) directly target STXBP1 3'UTR, decreasing STXBP1 gene expression by 0.06-0.84 fold (PMID: 37668872).

7. **Pathway Integration**: STXBP1 was identified as a hub gene related to copper metabolism in Parkinson's disease, suggesting potential involvement in neurodegenerative pathways (PMID: 39552415).

**Strength of Biological Evidence: STRONG**

The biological evidence is strong, supported by: (1) well-characterized molecular function in synaptic vesicle exocytosis; (2) multiple independent mechanisms of synaptic dysfunction identified in patient-derived neurons and animal models; (3) demonstration of haploinsufficiency as the primary pathogenic mechanism; (4) identification of novel protein interactions (Doc2, DDHD2, α-synuclein) with functional validation; (5) non-synaptic functions in dendritic growth and cytoskeletal organization; and (6) integration into multiple biological pathways relevant to neurodegeneration and memory.

## 5. OVERALL ASSESSMENT

**Overall Evidence Level: STRONG**

STXBP1 is a well-established neurodevelopmental disorder gene with compelling evidence across all three evidence categories.

**Key Strengths of the Evidence:**

1. **Large, consistent clinical cohorts**: Over 185 patients across multiple independent studies with remarkably consistent phenotypes (seizures in 95-100%, intellectual disability in 87-88%, developmental delay in 100%)

2. **De novo mutations in unaffected parents**: The predominance of de novo variants confirms pathogenicity and establishes autosomal dominant inheritance

3. **Genotype-phenotype correlations**: Natural history studies have identified specific correlations between variant type (truncating vs. missense) and clinical presentation (PMID: 38015929)

4. **Multiple model organisms with consistent phenotypes**: Heterozygous knockouts in mice, zebrafish, Drosophila, and C. elegans all recapitulate key human features (seizures, cognitive dysfunction, motor dysfunction)

5. **Mechanistic insights**: Multiple independent studies have identified specific synaptic dysfunction mechanisms (impaired inhibitory neurotransmission, defective microcircuit inhibition, reduced cortico-striatal transmission) that explain the seizure phenotype

6. **Functional validation**: Pharmacological rescue (levetiracetam in mice, clemizole/trazodone in zebrafish) and genetic rescue (wild-type STXBP1 restores function in humanized models) confirm pathogenicity

7. **Natural history data**: Long-term follow-up of 30 patients into adulthood and 162 patients across 1,281 cumulative patient-years provides robust phenotypic characterization

**Key Gaps or Limitations:**

1. **Limited enrollment in major international cohorts**: The studies do not explicitly indicate participation in large coordinated cohorts (UDN, GREGoR, DDD), which could provide additional standardized phenotypic data

2. **Incomplete variant classification**: While many studies report "pathogenic variants," specific variant details are sometimes limited, and the full spectrum of pathogenic variants (particularly rare missense variants) may not be fully characterized

3. **Limited therapeutic data**: While natural history studies document age-dependent anti-seizure medication response, prospective therapeutic trials are lacking

4. **Incomplete characterization of non-seizure phenotypes**: While movement disorders, behavioral problems, and autism spectrum features are documented, detailed characterization of these phenotypes and their natural history is limited

5. **Mechanistic heterogeneity**: Different studies have identified distinct mechanisms of synaptic dysfunction (inhibitory vs. excitatory, different interneuron subtypes), suggesting potential mechanistic heterogeneity that may correlate with variant type or location

## CONCLUSION

STXBP1 is a well-established autosomal dominant neurodevelopmental disorder gene with strong clinical, model organism, and biological evidence. Heterozygous pathogenic variants cause a severe neurodevelopmental encephalopathy characterized by early infantile seizures (95-100% of patients), severe intellectual disability (87-88%), developmental delay with regression (59%), and multiple neurological features including movement disorders, autism spectrum features, and behavioral problems. The consistent phenotype across large clinical cohorts, combined with robust animal models demonstrating seizures and cognitive dysfunction, and detailed mechanistic studies identifying impaired synaptic neurotransmission as the primary pathogenic mechanism, provide compelling evidence for STXBP1 as a major neurodevelopmental disorder gene. Future work should focus on genotype-phenotype correlations, therapeutic development targeting identified synaptic dysfunction mechanisms, and investigation of the potential connection between STXBP1 haploinsufficiency and later-life synucleinopathies.
