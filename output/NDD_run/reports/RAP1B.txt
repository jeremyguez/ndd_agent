# LITERATURE EVIDENCE REPORT: RAP1B AS A NEURODEVELOPMENTAL DISORDER GENE

## 1. GENE SUMMARY

RAP1B is a member of the RAS family of small GTPases that functions as a molecular switch regulating cell signaling, cytoskeletal remodeling, cell trafficking, and integrin activation (PMID: 39225097). RAP1B plays critical roles in multiple biological processes including platelet function, endothelial biology, immune cell regulation, and neurological development through activation of downstream signaling cascades including MEK/ERK and MAPK pathways.

## 2. CLINICAL EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Number of independent reports:** Four independent case reports/case series were identified.

**Total patient count:** Approximately 6 patients with RAP1B-related neurodevelopmental disorders across all studies.

**Most common phenotypes observed:**
- Thrombocytopenia (present in all cases)
- Neurodevelopmental delay/developmental delay (4/4 studies)
- Congenital malformations (4/4 studies)
- Dysmorphic features (3/4 studies)
- Behavioral/neurological features (3/4 studies)
- Microcephaly (2/4 studies)
- Learning difficulties (2/4 studies)
- Cardiovascular, genitourinary, and skeletal malformations (1 study with comprehensive phenotyping)

**Predominant variant type:** Missense variants exclusively. All reported variants are heterozygous missense mutations affecting conserved residues (Gly12 and Gly60 positions appear as recurrent hotspots). Variants are characterized as gain-of-function based on functional studies showing disruption of GTPase activity and increased RAP1B activation (PMID: 37850357, PMID: 35451551, PMID: 32627184).

**De novo status:** All identified variants are de novo, consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance with complete penetrance.

**Cohort origin:** No patients were identified as originating from known diagnostic cohorts (UDN, GREGoR, DDD, RUMC, GEL). Studies appear to be single-center or multi-center case reports without systematic cohort ascertainment.

**Strength of clinical evidence: MODERATE**

The clinical evidence is strengthened by: (1) consistent phenotypic presentation across independent cases; (2) recurrent hotspot mutations at Gly12 and Gly60 positions; (3) functional validation demonstrating gain-of-function mechanisms; (4) de novo status in all cases supporting pathogenicity. However, evidence is limited by: (1) small total patient number (n=6); (2) lack of systematic cohort studies; (3) incomplete variant characterization in some reports; (4) absence of large-scale population screening data.

## 3. MODEL ORGANISM EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Organisms studied:** Mouse models exclusively (8 studies identified).

**Key phenotypes from knockouts:**

*Neurological/behavioral phenotypes:*
- Impairment in retrieval of learned fear with decreased c-Fos expression in prelimbic cortex (PMID: 32944221)
- Normal acquisition of fear learning and innate fear responses, indicating selective forebrain RAP1B requirement for fear memory consolidation

*Developmental/growth phenotypes:*
- No embryonic lethality reported in conditional knockout models
- No Mendelian ratio deviations documented
- No infertility phenotypes reported

*Vascular and systemic phenotypes:*
- Impaired angiogenesis and altered endothelial function (PMID: 40508181)
- Reduced body weight, hepatic lipid accumulation, and improved metabolic parameters in liver-specific knockouts (PMID: 40083059)
- Altered calcium homeostasis and inflammatory responses

**Consistency with human phenotype:**

The mouse knockout studies demonstrate RAP1B's role in neurological function (fear memory), vascular development, and metabolic regulation. However, the conditional knockout models do not fully recapitulate the syndromic thrombocytopenia with neurodevelopmental delay observed in human patients. Notably, the mouse studies focus on loss-of-function phenotypes, while human disease appears driven by gain-of-function variants. One study (PMID: 26280580) examined a de novo dominant RAP1B mutation in a Kabuki syndrome-like phenotype with functional studies in zebrafish and patient cell lines demonstrating MEK/ERK signaling dysfunction, providing more direct evidence for dominant-negative effects.

**Strength of model organism evidence: MODERATE**

Strengths include: (1) multiple independent mouse models demonstrating RAP1B's role in neurological and vascular function; (2) specific evidence for forebrain RAP1B in learned fear; (3) no embryonic lethality, consistent with human heterozygous viability. Limitations include: (1) reliance on loss-of-function models rather than gain-of-function models matching human pathology; (2) lack of thrombocytopenia phenotype in mouse knockouts; (3) limited functional validation of specific human variants in animal models; (4) absence of comprehensive neurodevelopmental phenotyping in knockout mice.

## 4. BIOLOGICAL EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Known pathways and molecular function:**

RAP1B functions as a critical node in multiple signaling cascades:

1. **MEK/ERK signaling:** RAP1B activates downstream MEK/ERK pathway components, with functional studies demonstrating that RAP1B mutations disrupt this cascade (PMID: 26280580). Gain-of-function variants increase ERK phosphorylation and downstream transcriptional activity.

2. **Integrin and cytoskeletal signaling:** RAP1B regulates integrin activation and talin recruitment, controlling cell adhesion and cytoskeletal dynamics. Gain-of-function variants (G12E, G12V, G60R) enhance integrin activation in platelets and lymphocytes (PMID: 39225097).

3. **Calcium homeostasis:** RAP1B regulates store-operated calcium entry through Orai1-mediated mechanisms in endothelial cells, affecting inflammatory responses and vascular permeability (PMID: 40508181).

4. **Immune regulation:** RAP1B is activated downstream of IFITM2 in CD4+ T cells and activates ERK signaling to promote regulatory T cell (Treg) differentiation during sepsis (PMID: 40892462).

5. **Metabolic regulation:** RAP1B regulates hepatic lipid metabolism and inflammation through MAPK(p38) and NF-κB signaling (PMID: 40083059).

**Expression pattern:**

RAP1B is expressed broadly across tissues including brain, platelets, endothelium, and immune cells. The forebrain-specific knockout studies (PMID: 32944221) demonstrate selective neurological requirements, suggesting RAP1B has particular importance in cortical circuits involved in fear memory consolidation. The presence of neurodevelopmental phenotypes in human patients suggests RAP1B expression during embryonic neurogenesis and neural development is functionally important.

**Key experimental findings:**

- Functional studies in patient cell lines and zebrafish models demonstrate that RAP1B missense variants disrupt GTPase activity and increase constitutive activation (PMID: 26280580)
- Gain-of-function variants enhance talin recruitment and integrin activation, explaining thrombocytopenia through altered platelet function (PMID: 39225097)
- MEK/ERK pathway dysfunction is a consistent finding across functional studies
- F-actin polymerization and cell intercalation defects observed in zebrafish models suggest developmental roles in morphogenesis
- RAP1B regulates multiple signaling nodes (MAPK, NF-κB, PI3K/AKT) relevant to both immune and developmental processes

**Strength of biological evidence: STRONG**

Strengths include: (1) well-characterized molecular function as a GTPase regulating multiple signaling cascades; (2) functional validation of human variants in multiple experimental systems (patient cells, zebrafish, cell culture); (3) clear mechanistic link between gain-of-function variants and observed phenotypes (enhanced integrin activation, increased ERK signaling); (4) expression in relevant tissues (brain, platelets, endothelium); (5) multiple independent studies confirming pathway involvement. Limitations include: (1) limited direct study of RAP1B in embryonic neurogenesis; (2) incomplete characterization of how specific variants affect protein structure and dynamics; (3) most functional studies focus on somatic or in vitro systems rather than germline developmental contexts.

## 5. OVERALL ASSESSMENT

**Overall evidence level: MODERATE**

**Key strengths of the evidence:**

1. **Consistent clinical phenotype:** Six independent patients with de novo heterozygous RAP1B missense variants present with a recognizable syndrome combining thrombocytopenia, neurodevelopmental delay, congenital malformations, and dysmorphic features.

2. **Recurrent hotspot mutations:** Multiple independent cases carry variants at identical or adjacent residues (Gly12 and Gly60), strongly suggesting these positions are functionally critical and pathogenic.

3. **Functional validation:** Gain-of-function mechanism is supported by functional studies demonstrating increased GTPase activity, enhanced integrin activation, and MEK/ERK pathway dysregulation.

4. **Biological plausibility:** RAP1B's known roles in cytoskeletal regulation, integrin signaling, and developmental pathways provide clear mechanistic explanations for the observed phenotypes.

5. **De novo inheritance pattern:** All variants are de novo, consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance and supporting pathogenicity.

**Key gaps and limitations:**

1. **Small patient cohort:** Only 6 patients across 4 studies limits statistical power and phenotypic characterization. Larger cohort studies would strengthen evidence.

2. **Lack of systematic ascertainment:** No evidence of systematic screening in diagnostic cohorts (UDN, GREGoR, DDD). Cases appear to be identified opportunistically, potentially missing milder phenotypes.

3. **Incomplete genotype-phenotype correlation:** Phenotypic variability exists even among carriers of the same variant (e.g., c.178G>A appears in multiple studies with variable severity), suggesting incomplete penetrance or variable expressivity.

4. **Limited animal model validation:** Mouse knockout models do not fully recapitulate human gain-of-function phenotypes, particularly thrombocytopenia and neurodevelopmental features.

5. **Incomplete molecular characterization:** Some case reports lack detailed variant notation or functional validation data.

6. **Absence of loss-of-function variants:** All reported variants are missense/gain-of-function; no loss-of-function variants have been reported in patients, limiting understanding of haploinsufficiency.

**Conclusion:**

RAP1B is a credible neurodevelopmental disorder gene with autosomal dominant inheritance based on consistent clinical presentation across independent cases, recurrent hotspot mutations, and functional validation of gain-of-function mechanisms. The evidence is strengthened by biological plausibility and expression in relevant tissues. However, the moderate evidence level reflects the limited patient cohort size and lack of systematic population screening. Expansion of clinical ascertainment through diagnostic cohort screening and functional characterization of additional variants would substantially strengthen the evidence base and enable more precise genotype-phenotype correlation.
