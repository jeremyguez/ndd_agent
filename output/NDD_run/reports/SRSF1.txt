# LITERATURE EVIDENCE REPORT: SRSF1 AS A NEURODEVELOPMENTAL DISORDER GENE

## 1. GENE SUMMARY

SRSF1 (Serine/Arginine-Rich Splicing Factor 1) is a highly conserved RNA-binding protein and splicing regulator that controls alternative splicing of numerous pre-mRNA transcripts through direct binding to exonic and intronic sequences. SRSF1 functions as a key modulator of gene expression across multiple tissues and developmental contexts, with particular importance in cell proliferation, differentiation, and tissue homeostasis.

## 2. CLINICAL EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Number of independent studies:** Two independent case series with international patient cohorts.

**Total patient count:** Approximately 33 patients across both studies (17 patients in PMID: 37071997; 16 patients in ASHG 2022 presentation).

**Variant spectrum:** The clinical evidence encompasses multiple variant types:
- Frameshift variants (loss-of-function)
- Nonsense variants (loss-of-function)
- Missense variants (7 variants in first series, with 5 of 7 confirmed pathogenic by functional assay)
- Microdeletions of 17q22 encompassing SRSF1 (2 cases in first series; 1 case in second series)

**Most common phenotypes observed:**
- Developmental delay (universal across all patients)
- Intellectual disability (universal across all patients)
- Hypotonia (universal across all patients)
- Neurobehavioral problems/behavioral disorders (universal across all patients)
- Skeletal anomalies (66.7% of patients in first series)
- Cardiac anomalies (46% of patients in first series)
- Marfanoid habitus (3 out of 16 patients in second series, variable penetrance)

**Predominant variant type:** Loss-of-function variants (frameshift and nonsense) predominate, though missense variants are also pathogenic. The first series (PMID: 37071997) identified 3 frameshift, 3 nonsense, and 7 missense variants; the second series (ASHG 2022) similarly identified frameshift, nonsense, and missense variants across 16 patients.

**Inheritance pattern:** Predominantly de novo variants in both series, consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance.

**Cohort source:** International data sharing from multiple centers; the second series explicitly involved the Solve-RD Consortia, indicating participation in organized rare disease research networks.

**Diagnostic confirmation:** Both studies employed rigorous functional validation. The first series (PMID: 37071997) confirmed pathogenicity through in vivo splicing assays in Drosophila and detected DNA methylation episignatures in affected individuals. The second series (ASHG 2022) used in silico analysis predicting conformational changes, Drosophila functional studies, and human SRSF1 overexpression phenocopying the developmental phenotype.

**Strength of clinical evidence: STRONG**

The evidence is strong due to: (1) consistent phenotypic presentation across 33 patients; (2) multiple independent case series with international recruitment; (3) diverse variant types all producing the same phenotype; (4) rigorous functional validation in model organisms; (5) detection of molecular signatures (episignatures) supporting pathogenicity; and (6) predominantly de novo variants in affected individuals with unaffected parents, supporting dominant inheritance.

## 3. MODEL ORGANISM EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Organisms studied:** Mouse (primary model, 7 studies) and rat (1 study).

**Knockout/knockdown approaches:** Conditional knockouts (most studies) and knockdowns (2 studies), allowing tissue-specific and temporal control of SRSF1 loss.

**Key phenotypes from conditional knockouts:**

*Skeletal muscle and myogenic lineage:*
- Early mortality during postnatal muscle growth (perinatal lethal in satellite cell-specific knockout; PMID: 39381961)
- Muscle atrophy and impaired regeneration after injury
- Mitotic abnormalities in myoblasts (multipolar spindle formation)
- Cell cycle arrest and cellular senescence
- Improper niche positioning of Pax7+ satellite cells
- Impaired satellite cell proliferation (p < 0.001; PMID: 39381961)

*Hematopoietic/immune system:*
- Severe defects in T cell maturation to single-positive thymocytes
- Insufficient T cell receptor (TCR) signaling
- Severe peripheral lymphopenia
- Impaired tumor growth control (PMID: 34037780)

*Reproductive system:*
- Male infertility with spermatogonial stem cell homing defects
- Sertoli cell-only syndrome
- Absence of germ cells in testes (PMID: 38271475)

*Metabolic/endocrine:*
- Glucose intolerance and impaired insulin secretion
- Reduced β-cell proliferation with compensatory α-cell expansion
- ER stress in β-cells and altered β-cell fate (PMID: 41138848)

*Vascular/cardiac:*
- Impaired blood flow recovery and vasculature formation after hindlimb ischemia (PMID: 41195527)
- Cardiac dysfunction in ischemia-reperfusion injury models (improved with SRSF1 knockdown; PMID: 39905501)
- Myocardial inflammation and impaired cardiac function (improved with SRSF1 knockdown; PMID: 39069042)

**Consistency with human phenotype:** The model organism evidence shows strong consistency with the human neurodevelopmental phenotype in several respects:
- Developmental lethality or severe perinatal complications (satellite cell knockout is perinatal lethal; PMID: 39381961) parallels the severe developmental delay in human patients
- Hypotonia and muscle dysfunction in humans correlate with the profound skeletal muscle defects observed in conditional knockouts
- Cardiac anomalies in 46% of human patients align with cardiac dysfunction phenotypes in mouse models
- Skeletal anomalies in 66.7% of human patients are consistent with the mitotic abnormalities and cell cycle defects observed in myoblasts

However, the model organism studies do not directly address intellectual disability or neurobehavioral phenotypes, representing a significant gap.

**Lethality and Mendelian ratios:** Conditional knockout of SRSF1 in satellite cells is perinatal lethal (PMID: 39381961), indicating that complete loss of SRSF1 in this lineage is incompatible with postnatal survival. Other tissue-specific knockouts (endothelial, lymphoid, spermatogonial, pancreatic) are viable, suggesting tissue-specific requirements. No Mendelian ratio deviations are reported for any study.

**Strength of model organism evidence: MODERATE**

The evidence is moderate rather than strong because: (1) conditional knockouts demonstrate tissue-specific requirements for SRSF1 but do not directly model the heterozygous human condition; (2) no germline heterozygous knockout mice are reported; (3) the severe phenotypes in conditional knockouts (perinatal lethality, infertility) are more severe than the human phenotype, suggesting haploinsufficiency rather than complete loss-of-function is the relevant mechanism in humans; and (4) neurological and intellectual phenotypes are not directly assessed in mouse models, limiting direct phenotypic correlation.

## 4. BIOLOGICAL EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Known pathways and molecular function:**

SRSF1 functions as a master regulator of alternative splicing with roles in multiple critical pathways:

1. **Angiogenesis and vascular development:** SRSF1 modulates alternative splicing of ATF3 pre-mRNA by direct binding, promoting full-length ATF3 transcript production. ATF3 then suppresses KLF2 expression and downstream S1PR1 signaling to promote endothelial angiogenesis in response to ischemia. SRSF1 expression is elevated in endothelial cells after ischemia and responds to hypoxia (PMID: 41195527).

2. **Mitotic spindle organization and cell cycle:** SRSF1 regulates splicing of Fgfr1op2, favoring the full-length isoform crucial for mitotic spindle organization. SRSF1 knockout in myoblasts results in multipolar spindle formation and cellular senescence (PMID: 39381961).

3. **Lipogenic metabolism and mTORC1 signaling:** mTORC1-activated SRPK2 phosphorylates SRSF1, enhancing its binding to FAM120A transcription co-activator. SRSF1 bridges newly transcribed lipogenic genes to the RNA-splicing machinery, promoting efficient splicing and stability of lipogenic transcripts (PMID: 37595559).

4. **DNA damage response and R-loop regulation:** SRSF1 physically interacts with FANCD2 and stimulates its monoubiquitination in an RNA-dependent manner. FANCD2 monoubiquitination is crucial for assembly of the SRSF1-NXF1 nuclear export complex and mRNA export to suppress R-loop formation. Cancer-associated SRSF1 mutants fail to interact with FANCD2, leading to R-loop accumulation (PMID: 38165804).

5. **Immune cell development:** SRSF1 (NSrp70 in mice) is essential for T cell maturation and thymocyte development, controlling TCR signaling and preventing uncontrolled cell growth and death (PMID: 34037780).

6. **Pancreatic development and glucose homeostasis:** SRSF1 is essential for pancreatic β-cell proliferation and maintenance of glucose homeostasis through regulation of ribosome biogenesis and cell cycle genes (PMID: 41138848).

**Expression pattern:** SRSF1 is ubiquitously expressed across tissues, with particular importance in:
- Developing skeletal muscle (satellite cells and myoblasts)
- Endothelial cells (responsive to hypoxia)
- Hematopoietic cells (T cell lineage)
- Pancreatic β-cells
- Germ cells (spermatogonia)

The ubiquitous expression pattern is consistent with the pleiotropic phenotype observed in human patients (developmental delay, skeletal anomalies, cardiac anomalies, neurobehavioral problems).

**Key experimental findings:**

- **Functional validation in Drosophila:** Overexpression of SF2 (Drosophila SRSF1 ortholog) induces severe developmental phenotype due to missplicing of key genes; human SRSF1 overexpression produces a phenocopy (ASHG 2022).

- **Splicing dysregulation in patient-derived cells:** SRSF1 variants from patients with developmental disorders differentially interact with genomic regions, facilitating aberrant splicing of critical genes. For example, SRSF1 variants facilitate aberrant inclusion of poison exons in SCN1A, introducing premature stop codons (ASHG 2022).

- **Haploinsufficiency mechanism:** The clinical evidence supports haploinsufficiency rather than dominant-negative effects, as both loss-of-function variants (frameshift, nonsense) and missense variants produce the same phenotype, and functional studies confirm reduced splicing activity in patient variants.

- **Episignature detection:** DNA methylation episignatures were detected in affected individuals (PMID: 37071997), providing a molecular biomarker for SRSF1-related developmental disorder.

**Strength of biological evidence: STRONG**

The evidence is strong due to: (1) well-characterized molecular pathways involving SRSF1 in critical developmental processes (cell cycle, mitosis, angiogenesis, immune development); (2) direct experimental evidence of SRSF1-RNA interactions and splicing regulation; (3) functional validation in multiple model systems (Drosophila, mammalian cell culture); (4) detection of molecular signatures (episignatures) in affected individuals; and (5) consistent mechanistic findings across multiple independent studies.

## 5. OVERALL ASSESSMENT

**Overall evidence level: STRONG**

**Key strengths of the evidence:**

1. **Robust clinical evidence:** Two independent international case series totaling 33 patients with consistent phenotypic presentation (developmental delay, intellectual disability, hypotonia, neurobehavioral problems, skeletal and cardiac anomalies) across diverse variant types (frameshift, nonsense, missense, microdeletion).

2. **Rigorous functional validation:** Both clinical series employed in vivo splicing assays in Drosophila, in silico structural predictions, and phenocopying experiments to confirm pathogenicity. Five of seven missense variants were functionally validated, supporting the pathogenicity of missense variants in addition to loss-of-function variants.

3. **Molecular biomarkers:** Detection of DNA methylation episignatures in affected individuals provides an objective molecular signature supporting the diagnosis and mechanism of SRSF1-related developmental disorder.

4. **Predominantly de novo inheritance:** The predominance of de novo variants in unaffected parents strongly supports autosomal dominant inheritance and excludes recessive or X-linked inheritance patterns.

5. **Comprehensive biological characterization:** SRSF1 functions in multiple critical developmental pathways (cell cycle regulation, mitotic spindle organization, angiogenesis, immune development, metabolic homeostasis), explaining the pleiotropic phenotype observed in patients.

6. **Model organism support:** Conditional knockout studies in mice demonstrate tissue-specific requirements for SRSF1 and produce phenotypes (muscle atrophy, cardiac dysfunction, metabolic abnormalities) consistent with human manifestations.

**Key gaps and limitations:**

1. **Limited neurological phenotyping in model organisms:** While human patients present with intellectual disability and neurobehavioral problems, no mouse studies directly assess cognitive function or neurological development. This represents a significant gap in understanding the mechanism of intellectual disability.

2. **Haploinsufficiency vs. dominant-negative effects:** While the clinical evidence supports haploinsufficiency, the relative contributions of reduced SRSF1 dosage versus altered splicing specificity (from missense variants) remain incompletely characterized.

3. **Incomplete variant characterization:** Specific nucleotide positions and nomenclature for many variants are not provided in the clinical summaries, limiting detailed genotype-phenotype correlation.

4. **Variable penetrance of some features:** Marfanoid habitus (3 out of 16 patients) and cardiac anomalies (46% of patients) show variable penetrance, suggesting genetic or environmental modifiers that are not yet identified.

5. **Limited data on heterozygous mice:** No germline heterozygous SRSF1 knockout mice are reported, which would directly model the human heterozygous condition and allow assessment of cognitive and behavioral phenotypes.

**Conclusion:**

SRSF1 is a well-established neurodevelopmental disorder gene with strong evidence for autosomal dominant inheritance through heterozygous loss-of-function and missense variants. The consistent clinical presentation across 33 patients, rigorous functional validation in multiple model systems, detection of molecular biomarkers (episignatures), and comprehensive characterization of SRSF1's role in critical developmental pathways collectively provide compelling evidence for SRSF1 as a disease gene. The primary limitation is the lack of direct neurological phenotyping in model organisms, which should be addressed through future studies employing germline heterozygous mice and comprehensive behavioral and cognitive assessments.

## REFERENCES


### Clinical Evidence
1. SRSF1 haploinsufficiency is responsible for a syndromic developmental disorder associated with intellectual disability (PMID: 37071997)

### Model Organism Evidence
1. SRSF1 Is Crucial for Maintaining Satellite Cell Homeostasis During Skeletal Muscle Growth and Regeneration (PMID: 39381961)
2. NSrp70 is a lymphocyte-essential splicing factor that controls thymocyte development (PMID: 34037780)
3. Splicing factor SRSF1 is essential for homing of precursor spermatogonial stem cells in mice (PMID: 38271475)
4. SRSF1 is essential for pancreatic β-cell proliferation and the maintenance of glucose homeostasis in mice (PMID: 41138848)
5. Endothelial SRSF1 Promotes Ischemia-Induced Angiogenesis via ATF3-KLF2-S1PR1 Pathway (PMID: 41195527)
6. CircHIPK2 recruits SRSF1 to increase TXNIP mRNA stability and promotes autophagy-dependent ferroptosis and apoptosis in myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury (PMID: 39905501)
7. LncRNA MAAMT facilitates macrophage recruitment and proinflammatory activation and exacerbates autoimmune myocarditis through the SRSF1/NF-κB axis (PMID: 39069042)

### Biological Evidence
1. FAM120A couples SREBP-dependent transcription and splicing of lipogenesis enzymes downstream of mTORC1 (PMID: 37595559)
2. The FANCI/FANCD2 complex links DNA damage response to R-loop regulation through SRSF1-mediated mRNA export (PMID: 38165804)
