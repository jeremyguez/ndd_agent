# LITERATURE EVIDENCE REPORT: GPS2 AND NEURODEVELOPMENTAL DISORDER

## 1. GENE SUMMARY

GPS2 (G-Protein Pathway Suppressor 2) is a transcriptional coregulator and component of the NCoR/SMRT corepressor complex that functions as a non-proteolytic ubiquitination inhibitor, regulating K63-linked ubiquitination by the Ubc13 E2 enzyme. GPS2 has diverse roles in metabolic regulation, immune cell development, and cellular stress responses, with emerging evidence for importance in neuronal development and endothelial cell survival.

## 2. CLINICAL EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Number of independent reports:** 2 case series/reports

**Total patients:** 5 patients (4 patients in one series, 1 patient in another)

**Most common phenotypes observed:**
- Mental retardation/intellectual disability (5/5 patients, 100%)
- Developmental delay (5/5 patients, 100%)
- Microcephaly (3/5 patients, 60%)
- Dysmorphic features (3/5 patients, 60%)
- Intractable epilepsy (1/5 patients, 20%)
- Increased cancer risk (2/5 patients, 20%)

**Predominant variant type:** Microdeletions (not point mutations). Both reports describe large genomic deletions encompassing GPS2 along with multiple other genes (180 kb deletion in PMID: 19617690; 2.3 Mb deletion in PMID: 20111057). These are loss-of-function variants affecting multiple genes simultaneously.

**Cohort information:** No patients from established research cohorts (UDN, GREGoR, DDD, RUMC, GEL) were identified. One series was from Brazil (PMID: 19617690), with deletions found in ~1% of a 300-patient Brazilian cohort of mentally retarded individuals.

**Critical limitation:** Both clinical reports involve multi-gene microdeletions rather than GPS2-specific variants. While GPS2 was identified as a candidate gene within the deleted regions based on suspected tumor suppressor function and neuronal development roles, causality cannot be definitively attributed to GPS2 alone. No point mutations or GPS2-specific deletions in patients with neurodevelopmental phenotypes were identified in the literature.

**Strength of clinical evidence: LIMITED**

The clinical evidence is limited because: (1) only 5 total patients across 2 reports; (2) all variants are large multi-gene deletions, not GPS2-specific mutations; (3) no segregation data or functional validation linking GPS2 loss to the observed phenotypes; (4) one study (PMID: 20111057) screened 142 patients with developmental delay/epilepsy and found no pathogenic GPS2 mutations, suggesting GPS2 point mutations may not be a common cause of neurodevelopmental disease.

## 3. MODEL ORGANISM EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Organisms studied:** Mouse (6 studies with conditional or full knockouts)

**Key neurological and developmental phenotypes:**
- EC-specific GPS2 knockout (Gps2ECKO) mice are embryonic lethal at E16.5 due to defective vascularization and aberrant TNFR1-mediated endothelial cell death (PMID: 41507360)
- B cell developmental defects at multiple differentiation stages with enhanced signaling pathway activation (PMID: 28039360)
- Impaired beta cell function and glucose intolerance (PMID: 32937117)

**Lethality and viability:** Most conditional knockouts are viable (5/6 studies), but EC-specific GPS2 knockout is embryonic lethal, indicating GPS2 is essential for endothelial development and vascularization—processes critical for brain development.

**Mendelian ratio deviations:** Not reported in the available studies.

**Consistency with human phenotype:** The embryonic lethality of EC-specific GPS2 knockout is consistent with severe neurodevelopmental phenotypes observed in patients with 17p13.1 microdeletions. However, the mouse studies primarily focus on metabolic and immune functions rather than direct neuronal development. The B cell developmental defects and impaired glucose homeostasis are not directly comparable to the intellectual disability and epilepsy observed in human patients.

**Strength of model organism evidence: MODERATE**

The evidence is moderate because: (1) EC-specific GPS2 knockout demonstrates essential developmental function; (2) multiple conditional knockouts show GPS2 is important for cell-type-specific development and function; (3) however, no direct neuronal or brain-specific GPS2 knockout studies were identified; (4) the metabolic and immune phenotypes in mice do not directly recapitulate the intellectual disability and epilepsy seen in human patients; (5) no studies specifically examined neuronal development, synaptogenesis, or brain morphology.

## 4. BIOLOGICAL EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Known pathways and molecular function:**

GPS2 functions as a corepressor complex component with multiple regulatory roles:

1. **Ubiquitination regulation:** GPS2 inhibits K63-linked ubiquitination mediated by Ubc13 E2 enzyme, regulating translation of nuclear-encoded mitochondrial proteins and controlling ubiquitination of RNA-binding proteins and translation factors (PMID: 39094987)

2. **Protein stabilization:** GPS2 stabilizes critical proteins by preventing their proteasomal degradation:
   - Stabilizes HOIP (LUBAC catalytic subunit) by inhibiting K48-linked polyubiquitination, preventing TNF-induced endothelial cell death (PMID: 41507360)
   - Stabilizes ATF4 by impairing its interaction with BTRC E3 ligase (PMID: 40693356)
   - Stabilizes HIF-1α through RACK1-mediated interactions (PMID: 38942092)

3. **Transcriptional regulation:** GPS2 is a component of the NCoR/SMRT/HDAC3 corepressor complex that regulates chromatin occupancy and switches between repressive and activating functions (PMID: 40305047)

4. **Protein-protein interactions:** GPS2 mediates interactions between ANKRD26 and ETV6 in the NCoR complex, with relevance to thrombocytopenia pathogenesis (PMID: 39791724)

5. **Phase separation:** GPS2 undergoes liquid-liquid phase separation to form biomolecular condensates that regulate metabolic reprogramming (PMID: 40715488)

**Expression pattern and neurodevelopmental relevance:**

The biological evidence does not provide direct information on GPS2 expression in the developing brain or neuronal tissues. However, GPS2's roles in endothelial cell survival (PMID: 41507360), mitochondrial protein translation (PMID: 39094987), and stress response regulation suggest potential relevance to neuronal development and function. The embryonic lethality of EC-specific GPS2 knockout indicates GPS2 is critical for vascularization, which is essential for brain development.

**Key experimental findings:**

- GPS2 is essential for endothelial cell survival and vascularization during embryogenesis (PMID: 41507360)
- GPS2 regulates mitochondrial protein translation and antioxidant gene expression (PMID: 39094987)
- GPS2 is a component of the NCoR complex mediating multiple protein-protein interactions (PMID: 39791724, PMID: 22666460)
- GPS2 functions as a tumor suppressor in breast cancer and colorectal cancer through metabolic reprogramming (PMID: 38942092, PMID: 40715488)

**Strength of biological evidence: STRONG**

The biological evidence is strong because: (1) GPS2's molecular functions are well-characterized across multiple studies; (2) GPS2 is demonstrated to be essential for embryonic development (EC-specific knockout is embryonic lethal); (3) GPS2 regulates critical cellular processes including protein stability, ubiquitination, and transcription; (4) however, direct evidence for GPS2 function in neuronal development, brain morphogenesis, or synaptogenesis is lacking; (5) the connection between GPS2's known functions and the specific neurodevelopmental phenotypes (intellectual disability, epilepsy) observed in patients remains unclear.

## 5. OVERALL ASSESSMENT

**Overall evidence level: LIMITED**

**Key strengths of the evidence:**

1. **Biological function is well-characterized:** GPS2 has established roles in protein stability, ubiquitination regulation, and transcriptional control with demonstrated importance in embryonic development.

2. **Essential developmental function:** EC-specific GPS2 knockout is embryonic lethal, demonstrating GPS2 is critical for vascularization and embryonic development.

3. **Genomic localization in disease region:** GPS2 is located within the 17p13.1 microdeletion region associated with neurodevelopmental phenotypes in multiple patients.

4. **Candidate gene identification:** GPS2 was specifically identified as a tumor suppressor gene with suspected neuronal development function within the shortest region of overlap for 17p13.1 microdeletion syndrome (PMID: 20111057).

**Key gaps and limitations:**

1. **No GPS2-specific mutations in patients:** All clinical evidence involves large multi-gene microdeletions. No point mutations, frameshift mutations, or GPS2-specific deletions have been identified in patients with neurodevelopmental disorders. One study screening 142 patients with developmental delay/epilepsy found no pathogenic GPS2 mutations (PMID: 20111057).

2. **Small patient cohort:** Only 5 total patients across 2 reports, all with multi-gene deletions affecting 16-18 additional genes simultaneously.

3. **Lack of neuronal-specific studies:** No brain-specific or neuron-specific GPS2 knockout models have been reported. Model organism evidence focuses on metabolic, immune, and endothelial functions rather than neuronal development.

4. **Unclear causality:** The contribution of GPS2 loss versus loss of other genes in the deleted regions cannot be determined from the available clinical data.

5. **No functional validation:** No studies have demonstrated that GPS2 haploinsufficiency or loss-of-function specifically causes intellectual disability, developmental delay, or epilepsy.

**Conclusion:**

GPS2 is a biologically important gene with established roles in embryonic development, protein stability, and cellular stress responses. While GPS2 is located within a genomic region associated with neurodevelopmental phenotypes and was identified as a candidate gene based on suspected neuronal development function, the current evidence does not support GPS2 as a confirmed neurodevelopmental disorder gene. The clinical evidence is limited to 5 patients with large multi-gene microdeletions, and no GPS2-specific pathogenic variants have been identified in patients with neurodevelopmental disease. To establish GPS2 as a neurodevelopmental disorder gene, additional evidence would be needed including: (1) identification of GPS2-specific point mutations or deletions in patients with neurodevelopmental phenotypes; (2) functional studies demonstrating that GPS2 haploinsufficiency causes neuronal developmental defects; and (3) brain-specific or neuron-specific GPS2 knockout models showing neurodevelopmental abnormalities.
