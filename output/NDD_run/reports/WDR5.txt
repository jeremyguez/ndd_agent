# LITERATURE EVIDENCE REPORT: WDR5 AS A NEURODEVELOPMENTAL DISORDER GENE

## 1. GENE SUMMARY

WDR5 (WD Repeat Domain 5) is a WD40-repeat protein that serves as a core scaffolding component of histone H3 Lysine 4 (H3K4) methyltransferase complexes, including the WRAD complex and MLL/KMT2 family complexes. WDR5 is essential for establishing and maintaining H3K4 trimethylation (H3K4me3) marks at gene promoters, a critical epigenetic modification associated with active transcription and gene regulation.

## 2. CLINICAL EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Number of case reports/series:** 0

**Total patients:** 0

**Known cohort sources:** Not applicable

**Strength of clinical evidence: NONE**

No clinical case reports, case series, or patient data were identified in the literature search. There is currently no published evidence of WDR5 pathogenic variants in human patients with neurodevelopmental or other disorders.

## 3. MODEL ORGANISM EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Organisms studied:** Zebrafish, mouse

**Key neurological and developmental phenotypes:**

Mouse studies demonstrate that WDR5 is essential for early hematopoietic development. A conditional knockout mouse model showed embryonic lethality with sub-Mendelian ratios, defective erythropoiesis, and impaired hematopoietic stem and progenitor cell (HSPC) function (PMID: 37061728). Additionally, a full knockout mouse model revealed that WDR5 stability is regulated by the E3 ubiquitin ligase RNF220, with disruption leading to marked upregulation of Hox genes in the pons and disrupted Hox expression patterns in pontine nuclei, affecting neural circuit development in the hindbrain (PMID: 39526890). Notably, this RNF220 knockout was viable, suggesting that complete loss of WDR5 regulation can be tolerated postnatally.

Zebrafish studies provide more direct evidence of WDR5's role in genomic stability during development. Wdr5 full knockout in zebrafish embryos resulted in severe reduction of the HSPC pool in caudal hematopoietic tissue, accompanied by attenuated H3K4 methylation, p53-dependent apoptosis in HSPCs, abnormal R-loop accumulation, and impaired DNA damage response (PMID: 40112113). These findings establish WDR5-mediated H3K4 methylation as essential for maintaining genomic stability during rapid cell proliferation.

**Consistency with human neurodevelopmental phenotype:** The model organism evidence demonstrates WDR5's critical role in early development, particularly in hematopoiesis and hindbrain patterning. However, no human neurodevelopmental cases exist to directly compare phenotypes. The embryonic lethality observed in conditional mouse knockouts suggests that complete loss of WDR5 function is incompatible with normal development, which would be consistent with a dominant-negative or haploinsufficiency mechanism for heterozygous variants in humans.

**Strength of model organism evidence: MODERATE**

While the model organism data clearly establish WDR5's essential developmental role, the evidence is limited by: (1) lack of heterozygous knockout phenotype data in mice or zebrafish; (2) absence of studies specifically examining neurodevelopmental outcomes beyond hindbrain patterning; and (3) no published data on hypomorphic or missense variants that might model human heterozygous pathogenic variants.

## 4. BIOLOGICAL EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Known pathways and molecular function:**

WDR5 functions as a critical component of multiple histone methyltransferase complexes with diverse roles in gene regulation:

- **H3K4 methylation and transcriptional activation:** WDR5 is a core component of the WRAD complex and MLL/KMT2 family complexes that catalyze H3K4 trimethylation at gene promoters (PMID: 39779849, PMID: 36732025). H3Q5 histaminylation antagonizes WDR5 binding and inhibits H3K4 methyltransferase activity, demonstrating regulatory mechanisms controlling WDR5 function (PMID: 39779849).

- **Oncogenic pathways:** WDR5 interacts directly with MYC oncoproteins to regulate target gene selection through an avidity-based chromatin recognition mechanism (PMID: 26383167). In cancer contexts, WDR5 is recruited to specific promoters by other proteins: MBD2 recruits WDR5 to the ABCB1 promoter to activate drug transporter expression (PMID: 39350229); NSUN5 recruits WDR5 to the SMAD3 promoter to facilitate HCC metastasis (PMID: 39531371); and WDR5 is essential for maintaining glioblastoma cancer stem cells through WRAD complex assembly (PMID: 36732025).

- **Inflammatory and fibrotic pathways:** WDR5-dependent H3K4me3 mediates IL-4-induced macrophage differentiation and M2 macrophage phenotype establishment (PMID: 37149865). In cardiac fibrosis, the WDR5/MLL1 methyltransferase complex mediates H3K4 trimethylation that cooperates with PRMT5-mediated histone modifications at the α-SMA promoter downstream of TGF-β/Smad3 signaling (PMID: 38503742). WDR5 is also part of the STING-WDR5/DOT1L/IRF3-NLRP3 signaling pathway in hepatocyte pyroptosis and liver fibrosis (PMID: 37018971).

**Expression pattern and brain relevance:**

The biological evidence indicates WDR5 is broadly expressed and essential for normal development. The hindbrain-specific phenotypes observed in the RNF220 knockout mouse (PMID: 39526890) suggest particular importance in neural development, though WDR5 expression is not limited to the nervous system. The role of WDR5 in maintaining genomic stability during rapid cell proliferation (PMID: 40112113) is particularly relevant to neurodevelopment, where neural progenitor cells undergo extensive proliferation.

**Key experimental findings:**

- WDR5 is required for maintaining H3K4me3 marks at promoters of genes essential for stem cell function and self-renewal
- WDR5 function can be antagonized by histone monoaminylation, providing a regulatory mechanism
- WDR5 is recruited to specific genomic loci through protein-protein interactions with transcription factors and chromatin regulators
- Loss of WDR5 function leads to loss of H3K4me3 marks and reduced expression of target genes

**Strength of biological evidence: STRONG**

The biological evidence is comprehensive, with multiple independent studies demonstrating WDR5's essential role in H3K4 methylation, its recruitment to specific genomic loci, and its involvement in multiple developmental and disease pathways. However, the evidence is primarily from cancer and inflammatory disease contexts rather than neurodevelopmental disorders specifically.

## 5. OVERALL ASSESSMENT

**Overall evidence level: LIMITED**

**Key strengths:**
1. Strong biological evidence establishing WDR5 as an essential epigenetic regulator with clear molecular mechanisms
2. Model organism evidence demonstrating WDR5's critical role in early development and genomic stability
3. Evidence of WDR5's involvement in neural development (hindbrain patterning in mice)
4. Embryonic lethality in conditional knockouts suggesting haploinsufficiency or dominant-negative potential

**Key gaps and limitations:**
1. **Complete absence of clinical evidence:** No published case reports or case series of WDR5 pathogenic variants in human patients
2. **Lack of heterozygous knockout data:** No published studies examining phenotypes of heterozygous WDR5 loss in model organisms
3. **Limited neurodevelopmental specificity:** While WDR5 is essential for development, published evidence focuses primarily on hematopoiesis, hindbrain patterning, and cancer rather than broader neurodevelopmental outcomes
4. **No functional studies of missense variants:** No published data on how specific missense or loss-of-function variants might affect WDR5 protein function
5. **No expression data in developing human brain:** Limited information on WDR5 expression patterns during human neurodevelopment

**Conclusion:**

While WDR5 is clearly an essential gene with critical roles in early development and epigenetic regulation, there is currently insufficient clinical evidence to establish it as a neurodevelopmental disorder gene. The complete absence of published human cases, combined with the embryonic lethality observed in complete knockout models, suggests that if WDR5 variants do cause human neurodevelopmental disease, they would likely be rare and require functional characterization. Future studies should include systematic screening of WDR5 in neurodevelopmental disorder cohorts, functional analysis of identified variants, and generation of heterozygous mouse models to determine whether partial loss of WDR5 function can cause neurodevelopmental phenotypes in humans.
