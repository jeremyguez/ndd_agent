# LITERATURE EVIDENCE REPORT: HDAC2 AS A NEURODEVELOPMENTAL DISORDER GENE

## 1. GENE SUMMARY

HDAC2 (histone deacetylase 2) is a class I histone deacetylase that functions as a transcriptional co-repressor through removal of acetyl groups from histone and non-histone proteins. HDAC2 plays critical roles in chromatin remodeling, gene regulation, and cellular differentiation, with particular importance in neuronal development and embryonic stem cell lineage specification.

## 2. CLINICAL EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Number of independent studies:** 2 primary clinical studies identified HDAC2 variants in neurodevelopmental disorder patients.

**Total patient count:** Approximately 2 patients with de novo protein-truncating variants escaping nonsense-mediated decay (PTVesc), plus additional patients identified through aggregated developmental disorder analysis (exact number not specified in the ASHG 2015 abstract).

**Most common phenotypes observed:**
- Syndromic neurodevelopmental disorder
- Developmental delay
- Autism spectrum disorder
- Intellectual disability
- Epilepsy

**Predominant variant type:** Loss-of-function variants, specifically de novo protein-truncating variants. The ASHG 2022 study identified PTVesc mutations in HDAC2 through systematic analysis of 97,728 clinical exomes and 29,031 parent-child trios (ASHG 2022 - Systematic analysis of nonsense-mediated decay escaping protein-truncating variants). The ASHG 2015 study identified de novo rare variants in HDAC2 through aggregated analysis of 1,984 patients with undiagnosed developmental disorders, supported by de novo mutation data from approximately 5,500 NDD cases (ASHG 2015 - Aggregated analysis of developmental disorders reveals novel risk genes).

**Cohort information:** Studies appear to be from large clinical exome sequencing cohorts but specific diagnostic centers or known research networks (UDN, GREGoR, DDD, RUMC, GEL) are not explicitly identified in the provided abstracts.

**Strength of clinical evidence: MODERATE**

The evidence is strengthened by: (1) identification of de novo PTVesc mutations in independent patients with high phenotypic similarity; (2) systematic analysis across a large clinical exome cohort (97,728 exomes); (3) consistent phenotypic features across multiple patients (developmental delay, autism, intellectual disability, epilepsy). However, the evidence is limited by: (1) small absolute number of confirmed cases (2 patients with PTVesc); (2) lack of detailed clinical phenotyping in the abstracts; (3) absence of functional validation studies linking HDAC2 loss-of-function to the observed phenotypes.

## 3. MODEL ORGANISM EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Organisms studied:** Mouse (multiple studies) and human embryonic stem cells (hESCs).

**Key neurological phenotypes from knockouts:**

- **HDAC1 conditional knockout in mouse** (PMID: 35095417): Impaired neuronal differentiation in adult hippocampus and compromised neuronal differentiation in vitro upon viral infection-mediated deletion. The mouse remained viable, indicating that HDAC1/HDAC2 loss is not embryonic lethal.

- **HDAC2-mediated recruitment of METTL3 in human embryonic stem cells** (PMID: 41066234): HDAC2 knockout resulted in significantly impaired human embryonic stem cell differentiation, with reduced METTL3 chromatin binding, decreased m6A levels on HDAC2 target genes linked to lineage differentiation, and reduced RNA stability and translation of target genes. This directly demonstrates HDAC2's role in developmental differentiation.

**Consistency with human phenotype:** The model organism evidence shows that HDAC2 loss impairs neuronal and developmental differentiation, which is mechanistically consistent with the neurodevelopmental phenotypes observed in human patients (developmental delay, intellectual disability). The hESC data particularly supports a role for HDAC2 in early developmental processes.

**Strength of model organism evidence: MODERATE**

The evidence is strengthened by: (1) demonstration of impaired neuronal differentiation in relevant tissues (hippocampus); (2) direct evidence from human stem cells showing HDAC2 is required for proper developmental differentiation; (3) viable knockout animals, suggesting haploinsufficiency rather than complete loss is the relevant mechanism in humans. The evidence is limited by: (1) lack of a complete HDAC2 knockout mouse model with detailed neurodevelopmental phenotyping; (2) no reported seizures or autism-like behaviors in the model organisms; (3) most studies focus on HDAC1 or use conditional knockouts rather than germline loss-of-function models.

## 4. BIOLOGICAL EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Known pathways and molecular function:**

HDAC2 functions as a transcriptional co-repressor in multiple developmental and cellular pathways:

- **Stem cell differentiation:** HDAC2 recruits METTL3 to chromatin to regulate m6A methylation of genes controlling lineage differentiation (PMID: 41066234).

- **Histone acetylation and chromatin remodeling:** HDAC2 is recruited to gene promoters by transcription factors and co-factors (e.g., CBP, YY1) to regulate histone acetylation and RNA polymerase II binding (PMID: 35502657).

- **Transcriptional regulation:** HDAC2 acts as a transcriptional repressor of genes including PPAR-α and YTHDC1, and as an activator of genes like LAPTM4B (PMID: 38815686, PMID: 35974388, PMID: 39147759).

- **Post-translational modification:** HDAC2 is regulated by phosphorylation (by PIM1 kinase), ubiquitination (by HRD1 E3 ligase), and SUMOylation (substrate of SENP1 deSUMOylase), indicating its activity is tightly controlled (PMID: 39027246, PMID: 38815686, PMID: 38822351).

**Expression pattern:** While specific brain expression data are not detailed in the provided evidence, HDAC2's critical role in neuronal differentiation (PMID: 35095417) and embryonic stem cell differentiation (PMID: 41066234) indicates it is expressed and functionally important in developing neural tissues. GWAS enrichment analysis identified HDAC2 binding sites in kidney development genes (ASHG 2018 - Large-scale trans-ethnic genome-wide association study), suggesting HDAC2 is required for embryonic organ development broadly.

**Key experimental findings:**

- HDAC2 is essential for proper neuronal differentiation in the adult hippocampus and during in vitro neural differentiation.
- HDAC2 knockout impairs human embryonic stem cell differentiation through reduced METTL3 recruitment and m6A-mediated gene regulation.
- HDAC2 activity is reduced in severe asthma patients, leading to altered glucocorticoid receptor acetylation and inflammatory gene expression (PMID: 39170824), demonstrating that HDAC2 loss-of-function has functional consequences in human disease.
- HDAC2 is a direct substrate of multiple regulatory proteins (kinases, E3 ligases, deSUMOylases), indicating its activity is tightly controlled and sensitive to perturbation.

**Strength of biological evidence: STRONG**

The evidence is strengthened by: (1) multiple independent studies demonstrating HDAC2's requirement for neuronal and developmental differentiation; (2) detailed mechanistic studies showing HDAC2 recruits co-factors (METTL3) and regulates epigenetic marks (m6A, histone acetylation) critical for gene expression; (3) evidence from human cells (hESCs, asthma patients) showing HDAC2 loss has functional consequences; (4) GWAS enrichment suggesting HDAC2 is involved in developmental gene regulation. The evidence is limited by: (1) lack of studies specifically examining HDAC2 haploinsufficiency in neurodevelopmental contexts; (2) most functional studies examine complete loss rather than heterozygous loss-of-function.

## 5. OVERALL ASSESSMENT

**Overall evidence level: MODERATE**

**Key strengths of the evidence:**

1. **De novo loss-of-function variants in patients with neurodevelopmental phenotypes:** Two independent patients with de novo PTVesc mutations in HDAC2 identified through systematic analysis of 97,728 clinical exomes, with high phenotypic similarity scoring and consistent developmental phenotypes (ASHG 2022).

2. **Mechanistic plausibility:** HDAC2 is a histone modifier with well-established roles in neuronal differentiation and developmental gene regulation. Loss of HDAC2 impairs neuronal differentiation in hippocampus and embryonic stem cell differentiation, directly supporting a neurodevelopmental mechanism (PMID: 35095417, PMID: 41066234).

3. **Consistent phenotypic features:** Patients identified across multiple studies share core features of developmental delay, intellectual disability, autism, and epilepsy, suggesting a coherent clinical syndrome.

4. **Functional evidence from human cells:** HDAC2 knockout in human embryonic stem cells demonstrates impaired developmental differentiation with reduced m6A-mediated gene regulation, providing direct evidence of HDAC2's role in human developmental processes.

**Key gaps and limitations:**

1. **Small number of confirmed cases:** Only 2 patients with confirmed de novo PTVesc mutations; additional patients from the ASHG 2015 aggregated analysis are not individually characterized.

2. **Lack of functional validation:** No studies have directly tested whether the specific PTVesc variants identified in patients cause loss-of-function or impair HDAC2's role in neuronal differentiation.

3. **No haploinsufficiency studies:** Most model organism and functional studies examine complete loss of HDAC2; limited evidence specifically addresses whether heterozygous loss-of-function (as would occur with de novo dominant mutations) is sufficient to cause neurodevelopmental disease.

4. **Limited clinical detail:** The clinical abstracts provide limited phenotypic detail, severity assessment, or natural history information.

5. **Absence of segregation data:** No family studies demonstrating segregation of HDAC2 variants with disease in affected families.

**Conclusion:**

HDAC2 is a plausible neurodevelopmental disorder gene with moderate evidence supporting autosomal dominant inheritance of loss-of-function variants. De novo protein-truncating variants have been identified in patients with syndromic neurodevelopmental disorder through large-scale exome analysis, and HDAC2's established role in neuronal and developmental differentiation provides mechanistic support. However, the evidence would be strengthened by: (1) identification of additional independent cases; (2) functional studies demonstrating that patient variants impair HDAC2 function; and (3) detailed clinical characterization of affected individuals to define the associated phenotypic spectrum.
