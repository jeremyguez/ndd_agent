# LITERATURE EVIDENCE REPORT: SLC18A3 AND NEURODEVELOPMENTAL DISORDER

## 1. GENE SUMMARY

SLC18A3 encodes the vesicular acetylcholine transporter (VAChT), a member of the SLC18 family within the Major Facilitator Superfamily that mediates active transport of acetylcholine into synaptic vesicles for exocytotic release (PMID: 23506877). SLC18A3 is a critical component of cholinergic neurotransmission, functioning alongside CHAT and SLC5A7 in acetylcholine synthesis and packaging at the neuromuscular junction and throughout the central and peripheral nervous systems.

## 2. CLINICAL EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Number of Studies and Patient Count:**
One major case series was identified reporting 41 patients with recurrent genomic rearrangements of 10q11.21q11.23 that include SLC18A3 (PMID: 21948486).

**Predominant Variant Type:**
Structural variants (deletions and duplications) rather than point mutations. Deletions ranged from 0.3–12 Mb and were mediated by complex low-copy repeats. Duplications were also observed with variable size ranges.

**Most Common Phenotypes Observed:**
Deletion carriers (n=41) presented with a constellation of neurodevelopmental and systemic features including:
- Developmental delay and intellectual disability (core features)
- Hypotonia
- Neurological: epilepsy, ataxia, nystagmus, ptosis, dysphagia
- Autonomic/GI: sleep apnea, chronic constipation, gastroesophageal reflux, vesicoureteral reflux

Duplication carriers showed variable clinical features with developmental delay and/or intellectual disability in some cases, suggesting incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity.

**Important Clinical Observation:**
The study noted that deletions were identified in apparently phenotypically normal carrier parents, indicating that heterozygous carriers may be phenotypically variable or have mild manifestations (PMID: 21948486).

**Cohort Information:**
No indication that patients were from established research cohorts (UDN, GREGoR, DDD, RUMC, GEL).

**Strength of Clinical Evidence: MODERATE**

The evidence is based on a single, reasonably sized case series (n=41) with well-documented phenotypes and clear association with genomic rearrangements. However, the clinical evidence is limited by: (1) the structural nature of variants (which typically involve multiple genes in the 10q11.21q11.23 region, including CHAT); (2) lack of point mutation data specific to SLC18A3; (3) variable expressivity and incomplete penetrance that complicate genotype-phenotype correlation; and (4) inability to definitively attribute phenotypes to SLC18A3 alone versus other genes in the deleted/duplicated region.

## 3. MODEL ORGANISM EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Organisms Studied:**
Mouse (Mus musculus)

**Key Phenotypes from Knockouts:**
Complete knockout of SLC18A3 in mice results in embryonic lethality, indicating that the gene is essential for early development (PMID: 31059209). The study noted that SLC18A3 variants lead to fetal akinesia deformation sequence early in pregnancy, suggesting that loss of acetylcholine transport capacity severely impairs motor development and neuromuscular function in utero.

**Mendelian Ratio:**
Lethal phenotype with embryonic lethality observed.

**Consistency with Human Phenotype:**
The embryonic lethality in homozygous knockout mice is consistent with the severe neurodevelopmental and neuromuscular manifestations observed in human deletion carriers. However, human patients are heterozygous (with one functional copy remaining), whereas the mouse model represents complete loss of function. The fetal akinesia phenotype in mice aligns conceptually with the hypotonia, developmental delay, and motor dysfunction observed in human patients.

**Strength of Model Organism Evidence: MODERATE**

While the embryonic lethality demonstrates that SLC18A3 is essential for development, the evidence is limited by: (1) lack of heterozygous knockout data that would more directly model human autosomal dominant deletions; (2) limited detail on the specific neurological phenotypes beyond lethality; and (3) absence of data on partial loss-of-function models that might better recapitulate the human clinical presentation.

## 4. BIOLOGICAL EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Known Pathways and Molecular Function:**

SLC18A3 is a core component of the cholinergic synapse pathway. It functions as the vesicular acetylcholine transporter, working in concert with CHAT (choline acetyltransferase) and SLC5A7 (choline transporter) to synthesize and package acetylcholine into synaptic vesicles (PMID: 23506877). This pathway is essential for neuromuscular transmission and central cholinergic signaling.

SLC18A3 is regulated by DDAH1 through HIF-1α signaling during neural stem cell differentiation toward cholinergic neurons, particularly in the context of neural repair after stroke (PMID: 38799640).

**Expression Pattern and Neurodevelopmental Relevance:**

SLC18A3 is expressed in cholinergic neurons throughout the central and peripheral nervous systems. Evidence of neurodevelopmental relevance includes:

- **Brain and neural development:** SLC18A3 is regulated during cholinergic neurogenesis and neural repair (PMID: 38799640). Expression is significantly altered in human iPS cell-derived motor neurons exposed to developmental toxins (arsenic), where downregulation impairs the cholinergic synapse pathway (PMID: 37624150).

- **Neuromuscular junction:** SLC18A3 mutations cause congenital myasthenic syndromes with both neuromuscular and CNS manifestations, demonstrating its critical role in neuromuscular transmission (PMID: 29874875).

- **Autonomic nervous system:** SLC18A3 is expressed in sympathetic chain ganglia and marks cholinergic neuronal populations. In diabetes, cholinergic neurons expressing SLC18A3 undergo phenotypic shifts toward noradrenergic identity, indicating altered neuronal identity in autonomic dysfunction (PMID: 40667173).

- **Microbiota-dependent regulation:** SLC18A3 expression in the urinary bladder is significantly regulated by microbiota composition (fold change > ±2; FDR p-value < 0.01), suggesting that the cholinergic system is sensitive to environmental and microbial factors during development (PMID: 32192034).

**Key Experimental Findings:**

1. **Toxin sensitivity:** SLC18A3 transcript levels are downregulated following arsenic exposure in developing motor neurons, and microplastic exposure significantly downregulates SLC18A3 at both transcript and protein levels in mouse brain, leading to reduced acetylcholine concentration and cognitive dysfunction (PMID: 38484610, PMID: 37624150).

2. **Biomarker associations:** SLC18A3 expression is significantly altered in obstructive sleep apnea patients (p = 0.0006) and identified as a possible biomarker for severe OSA (PMID: 24732660), which is notably one of the phenotypes observed in the clinical deletion series.

3. **Pathway integration:** SLC18A3 participates in protein-protein interaction networks with other solute carrier family members and is part of broader neuromuscular synaptic transmission gene sets (PMID: 35957868).

**Strength of Biological Evidence: STRONG**

The biological evidence is robust, demonstrating: (1) well-characterized molecular function as the vesicular acetylcholine transporter; (2) critical role in neuromuscular and central cholinergic transmission; (3) expression in multiple neurodevelopmentally relevant tissues (brain, motor neurons, autonomic ganglia); (4) sensitivity to developmental toxins and environmental factors; (5) regulation during neural differentiation and repair; and (6) association with clinical phenotypes (sleep apnea, cognitive dysfunction). The convergence of multiple independent studies on SLC18A3's role in cholinergic neurotransmission and its vulnerability to developmental insults provides strong mechanistic support for its involvement in neurodevelopmental disorders.

## 5. OVERALL ASSESSMENT

**Overall Evidence Level: MODERATE**

**Key Strengths:**

1. **Clear molecular function:** SLC18A3's role as the vesicular acetylcholine transporter is well-established and directly relevant to neuromuscular and CNS function.

2. **Consistent clinical phenotypes:** The neurodevelopmental features observed in deletion carriers (developmental delay, intellectual disability, hypotonia, seizures, motor dysfunction) are mechanistically consistent with impaired cholinergic neurotransmission.

3. **Developmental essentiality:** Embryonic lethality in homozygous knockout mice demonstrates that SLC18A3 is essential for development, supporting its role in neurodevelopmental disorders.

4. **Pathway convergence:** Multiple independent studies identify SLC18A3 as a critical node in cholinergic neurotransmission, with expression altered in relevant developmental contexts (neural differentiation, motor neuron development, autonomic development).

5. **Phenotype-biomarker correlation:** SLC18A3 expression changes are associated with clinical phenotypes observed in the deletion series (e.g., sleep apnea).

**Key Gaps and Limitations:**

1. **Lack of point mutation data:** The clinical evidence is based entirely on structural variants (deletions/duplications) that involve multiple genes in the 10q11.21q11.23 region. No pathogenic point mutations in SLC18A3 alone have been reported, making it difficult to establish SLC18A3-specific pathogenicity independent of neighboring genes (particularly CHAT).

2. **Variable expressivity and incomplete penetrance:** The observation that apparently normal parents carry deletions complicates interpretation of causality and suggests that SLC18A3 haploinsufficiency alone may not fully explain the phenotype.

3. **Limited heterozygous model data:** Mouse studies focus on complete knockout; heterozygous knockout data would more directly model human autosomal dominant deletions.

4. **Small clinical cohort:** Only one case series with 41 patients; replication in independent cohorts would strengthen evidence.

5. **Confounding from multi-gene deletions:** The 10q11.21q11.23 region contains multiple genes; phenotypes may result from cumulative effects rather than SLC18A3 alone.

**Conclusion:**

SLC18A3 is a neurodevelopmentally important gene encoding the vesicular acetylcholine transporter with strong biological evidence for involvement in cholinergic neurotransmission, motor development, and autonomic function. Clinical evidence from a moderate-sized deletion series demonstrates association with neurodevelopmental delay, intellectual disability, and motor dysfunction. However, the evidence for SLC18A3 as an autosomal dominant neurodevelopmental disorder gene is limited by the absence of point mutation data and the confounding effects of multi-gene deletions. Future identification of heterozygous point mutations in SLC18A3 in patients with isolated neurodevelopmental phenotypes, combined with functional studies of haploinsufficiency, would substantially strengthen the evidence for SLC18A3 as a monogenic neurodevelopmental disorder gene.
