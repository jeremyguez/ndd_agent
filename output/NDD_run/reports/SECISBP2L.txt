# LITERATURE EVIDENCE REPORT: SECISBP2L AS A NEURODEVELOPMENTAL DISORDER GENE

## 1. GENE SUMMARY

SECISBP2L (SECIS-binding protein 2-like) is a paralogue of SECIS-binding protein 2 (SBP2) that functions as a selenocysteine incorporation factor required for the translation of selenoproteins, a class of proteins essential for antioxidant defense and metabolic regulation. The gene plays a critical role in selenoprotein synthesis, particularly in tissues with high metabolic demands such as the central nervous system and oligodendrocytes.

**Strength of clinical evidence: NONE**

No clinical case reports, case series, or patient cohorts with SECISBP2L pathogenic variants have been identified in the literature. The absence of clinical evidence represents a significant gap in establishing SECISBP2L as a neurodevelopmental disorder gene in humans.

## 3. MODEL ORGANISM EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Organisms studied:** Zebrafish and mouse

**Key phenotypes from knockouts:**

In zebrafish (PMID: 35210313), full knockout of secisbp2l alone resulted in retention of the full complement of selenoproteins, indicating functional redundancy with sbp2. However, double mutants (sbp2 -/- secisbp2l -/-) completely prevented selenoprotein synthesis and resulted in juvenile lethality at 14 days post-fertilization. Single sbp2 -/- mutants showed sensitivity to oxidative stress and elevated Vtg1 stress marker expression, suggesting impaired antioxidant capacity.

In mice (PMID: 35760530), conditional knockout of Secisbp2l in the oligodendrocyte lineage produced neurologically relevant phenotypes including decreased DIO2 (deiodinase 2) protein levels, reduced T3 (active thyroid hormone) in the central nervous system, impaired oligodendrocyte differentiation, hypomyelination, and motor deficits in both sexes. Notably, these deficits were rescued by T3 or T3 analogs but not T4, demonstrating a specific requirement for SECISBP2L-mediated selenoprotein synthesis in thyroid hormone metabolism within the CNS.

**Consistency with human phenotype:** The mouse conditional knockout phenotype is highly consistent with neurodevelopmental dysfunction, featuring myelination defects and motor impairment. However, the absence of human clinical cases prevents direct phenotypic correlation.

**Strength of model organism evidence: STRONG**

The model organism evidence is robust, demonstrating essential roles for SECISBP2L in selenoprotein synthesis and CNS development. The tissue-specific conditional knockout approach in mice provides clear mechanistic insight into neurodevelopmental pathology.

## 4. BIOLOGICAL EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Known pathways and molecular function:**

SECISBP2L functions as a SECIS-binding protein required for selenocysteine incorporation into selenoproteins. The gene is a paralogue of SBP2, and the two proteins exhibit functional redundancy in some contexts but non-redundant roles in others. The zebrafish studies (PMID: 35210313) demonstrate that while secisbp2l alone is dispensable, combined loss of both sbp2 and secisbp2l is lethal, indicating overlapping but distinct functions.

**Expression pattern and brain/neurodevelopmental relevance:**

SECISBP2L is highly expressed in differentiating oligodendrocytes (PMID: 35760530), a cell type critical for myelination and CNS development. The gene is also identified in hippocampal expression studies related to cognitive function (PMID: 37739108). Additionally, GWAS and eQTL analyses have identified SECISBP2L as a candidate gene in lung cancer susceptibility (PMID: 28604730, PMID: 39092217), and cross-trait GWAS analysis revealed pleiotropic effects on thyroid function and headache (PMID: 36672757), suggesting broader metabolic and neurological relevance.

**Key experimental findings:**

- SECISBP2L is essential for DIO2 translation in oligodendrocytes, linking selenoprotein synthesis to thyroid hormone metabolism in the CNS (PMID: 35760530)
- Double knockout of sbp2 and secisbp2l in zebrafish completely abolishes selenoprotein synthesis and causes perinatal lethality (PMID: 35210313)
- SECISBP2L functions as a hub gene in acute myeloid leukemia treatment response networks (PMID: 38069522), suggesting broader roles in cellular regulation
- The gene is identified as a prognostic RNA-binding protein in lung squamous cell carcinoma with regulatory networks affecting multiple cellular pathways (PMID: 34931870)

**Strength of biological evidence: STRONG**

The biological evidence is comprehensive, demonstrating clear molecular function in selenoprotein synthesis with specific relevance to CNS development through oligodendrocyte differentiation and thyroid hormone metabolism.

## 5. OVERALL ASSESSMENT

**Overall evidence level: LIMITED**

**Key strengths:**
- Robust model organism evidence demonstrating essential roles in CNS development, myelination, and motor function
- Clear molecular mechanism linking SECISBP2L to selenoprotein synthesis and thyroid hormone metabolism
- Tissue-specific conditional knockout studies in mice providing mechanistic insight into neurodevelopmental pathology
- Consistent functional redundancy patterns between SECISBP2L and SBP2 across model systems

**Key gaps and limitations:**
- Complete absence of human clinical evidence: no case reports, case series, or patient cohorts with SECISBP2L pathogenic variants
- No evidence for autosomal dominant inheritance patterns in humans
- No data on heterozygous variant pathogenicity in humans
- Lack of functional studies examining how specific human variants might impair SECISBP2L function
- Limited understanding of whether heterozygous loss-of-function variants would produce phenotypes given apparent functional redundancy with SBP2

**Conclusion:**

While SECISBP2L demonstrates strong biological and model organism evidence for essential roles in CNS development, myelination, and selenoprotein synthesis, there is currently insufficient clinical evidence to establish it as a neurodevelopmental disorder gene associated with heterozygous pathogenic variants in humans. The complete absence of reported human cases, despite the availability of large genomic databases and the identification of SECISBP2L in multiple GWAS studies, suggests either that heterozygous variants do not cause clinically recognizable neurodevelopmental phenotypes in humans, or that such cases have not yet been systematically identified and reported. Future studies should focus on screening neurodevelopmental disorder cohorts for SECISBP2L variants and characterizing their functional consequences.
