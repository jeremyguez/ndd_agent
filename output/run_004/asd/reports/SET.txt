SET GENE: LITERATURE EVIDENCE REPORT FOR AUTISM SPECTRUM DISORDER (HETEROZYGOUS / AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT)

1. GENE SUMMARY
SET encodes a multifunctional nuclear protein, also known as template-activating factor I beta (TAF-I beta) or I-2(PP2A). It acts as a histone chaperone and chromatin remodeling factor and as a potent endogenous inhibitor of protein phosphatase 2A (PP2A) (PMID: 9597000; PMID: 27351675). The protein is ubiquitously expressed and conserved from yeast to human.

2. CLINICAL EVIDENCE SUMMARY
- Independent case reports/case series: 0 were found.
- Total patients: none identified.
- Phenotypes: no patient phenotypes were reported, so no autism, neurodevelopmental or other clinical features can be attributed to SET variants from this dataset.
- Variant type: not applicable. No heterozygous LoF, missense or other germline variants were reported.
- Known cohorts (UDN, GREGoR, DDD, RUMC, GEL): none identified.
- Strength of clinical evidence: NONE.

3. MODEL ORGANISM EVIDENCE SUMMARY
- Organisms studied: no knockout or other model organism studies were returned. The only animal data are rat expression studies in liver and hepatoma (PMID: 11078917), and mouse tissue expression (PMID: 9597000). Neither tests a SET loss-of-function phenotype.
- Key phenotypes (neurological, lethality, Mendelian ratios): no data.
- Consistency with human phenotype: cannot be assessed.
- Strength of model organism evidence: NONE.

4. BIOLOGICAL EVIDENCE SUMMARY
- Pathways and molecular function: SET inhibits PP2A and acts as a histone-binding chromatin remodeler (PMID: 9597000). Its promoter contains a functional NFkB-responsive element, and NFkB upregulates SET isoform 1 but not isoform 2 (PMID: 27351675). SET is a direct target of miR-502-3P in hepatocellular carcinoma (PMID: 27330307). The brain-specific SET/NAP family member MB20 binds histones and has been proposed to modulate nucleosome structure and gene expression during brain development (PMID: 11161794).
- Expression: SET is ubiquitous and predominantly nuclear, with stable levels across the cell cycle (PMID: 9597000). In Alzheimer's disease and Down syndrome brain, SET is elevated and mislocalized to the cytoplasm, where it inhibits PP2A and contributes to tau pathology (PMID: 27351675). This indicates relevance to neuronal biology, but only in neurodegeneration and not in neurodevelopment or autism.
- Key experimental findings: most of the data concern SET overexpression and gain of function in cancer.
  - In glioblastoma cells, SET knockdown increased apoptosis and reduced proliferation and migration (PMID: 27698810).
  - SET is upregulated in colorectal and oral squamous carcinoma, and knockdown reduced proliferation by about 20% and increased apoptosis by about 18% (PMID: 21725597).
  - High SET expression predicted poorer overall survival and lower complete remission in AML (141 patients, OS P=0.001, PMID: 24857208; 59 patients, OS P=0.021, PMID: 29534404).
  - SET is overexpressed in pediatric ALL (96.5% of B-ALL, 93% of T-ALL) (PMID: 22677993).
  - SET is upregulated in hepatoma and regenerating liver (PMID: 11078917).
- Relevance to the disease mechanism: these findings support an oncogenic, dosage-increase role. They do not address haploinsufficiency or heterozygous variants in neurodevelopmental disorders, and no autism-related experiments were provided.
- Strength of biological evidence: LIMITED. The biology is plausible and the protein is well characterized, but the data do not bear on autism or neurodevelopment.

5. OVERALL ASSESSMENT
- Overall evidence level: INSUFFICIENT.
- Key strengths: SET has well-defined molecular functions (PP2A inhibition, histone chaperoning). It is implicated in brain pathology (Alzheimer's disease, Down syndrome) and is regulated by inflammatory and oxidative stress pathways (PMID: 27351675).
- Key gaps and limitations:
  - There are no patient reports, no de novo or inherited variant data, and no cohort data.
  - There are no model organism loss-of-function studies.
  - The biological literature is dominated by cancer overexpression studies, which are not informative for heterozygous pathogenic variants in autism.
  - There is no evidence on variant mechanism (LoF vs missense).
- Conclusion: On the evidence provided, there is no support for SET as an autosomal dominant autism spectrum disorder gene. The available literature shows SET is biologically important and has some links to neuronal pathology, but it consists entirely of cancer and neurodegeneration expression and function studies. Any gene-disease assertion would require identification of affected individuals with heterozygous SET variants and supporting functional or model organism data.
