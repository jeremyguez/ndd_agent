# DLGAP4 – Literature Evidence Report: Candidate Gene for Epileptic Encephalopathy (Autosomal Dominant / Heterozygous)

---

## 1. GENE SUMMARY

DLGAP4 (Discs Large-Associated Protein 4; also known as SAPAP4) encodes a postsynaptic density scaffold protein that links glutamate receptors to downstream signaling proteins and cytoskeletal components at excitatory synapses, functioning as a core component of the PSD95–SAPAP–SHANK complex (PMID: 28870203). Beyond its canonical synaptic role, DLGAP4 has recently been shown to play a critical role during cortical neurodevelopment, specifically in maintaining ventricular surface integrity and regulating neuronal migration (PMID: 35585091).

---

## 2. CLINICAL EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Number of independent reports:** Three independent lines of clinical evidence have been identified, each describing distinct patient(s) and distinct variant types.

**Total approximate number of patients:** Approximately 4 individuals across all reports: one patient with subcortical heterotopia (PMID: 35585091), an unspecified number of affected individuals across five generations of a family with cerebellar ataxia (PMID: 24986922), and two monozygotic twin sisters with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) (ASHG 2019 – DLGAP4: A novel candidate gene for autism spectrum disorders).

**Most common phenotypes observed:** The phenotypes reported across cases are heterogeneous and include:
- Subcortical heterotopia with an EML1-like phenotype (implying epilepsy and intellectual disability) (PMID: 35585091)
- Early-onset cerebellar ataxia (PMID: 24986922)
- Autism spectrum disorder (ASHG 2019)

Notably, epileptic encephalopathy *per se* has not been reported as a primary diagnosis in any published case. Epilepsy is implied secondarily in the heterotopia case (PMID: 35585091), as subcortical heterotopia is a well-established cause of drug-resistant epilepsy, but it is not explicitly documented.

**Predominant variant type:** All reported variants are structural or chromosomal in nature — specifically, balanced chromosomal translocations disrupting or truncating DLGAP4 (PMID: 24986922; ASHG 2019) and an uncharacterized novel sequence variant in one patient (PMID: 35585091). No point loss-of-function (LoF) variants (nonsense, frameshift, canonical splice-site) or pathogenic missense variants have been reported in the peer-reviewed literature to date.

**Cohort origin:** None of the patients are reported to originate from established large-scale diagnostic cohorts such as UDN, GREGoR, DDD, RUMC, or GEL.

**Strength of clinical evidence: LIMITED**
The clinical evidence is limited by very small patient numbers, phenotypic heterogeneity across reports, absence of recurrent point variants, and the lack of formal variant classification according to ACMG/AMP criteria in any of the reports. The connection to epileptic encephalopathy specifically is inferential rather than directly documented.

---

## 3. MODEL ORGANISM EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Organisms studied:** All model organism studies have been conducted in mouse (*Mus musculus*).

**Key phenotypes from knockouts and knockdowns:**

- *Heterozygous knockout mice* show developmental defects in the dorsal telencephalon, disruption of ventricular zone integrity, and neuronal migration defects — findings directly relevant to the subcortical heterotopia phenotype observed in the human case (PMID: 35585091). This is particularly notable because heterozygous loss recapitulates a neurodevelopmental phenotype, consistent with an autosomal dominant mechanism.

- *In utero electroporation knockdown* in mice produces ventricular surface phenotypes including altered progenitor cell dynamics, morphology, and proliferation, as well as disruption of radial glial cell adherens junction components and actin cytoskeleton dynamics. Wild-type but not patient-mutant DLGAP4 rescued the knockdown phenotype, providing functional evidence for variant pathogenicity (PMID: 35585091).

- *Full knockout (homozygous) mice* are viable and display cognitive deficits, impaired vocal communication, impaired social interaction, and autistic-like behavior, along with dramatic changes in synapse morphology, synaptic function, and plasticity (PMID: 30664629). A second full knockout study demonstrated reduced dendritic spine density in the prefrontal cortex, abnormal postsynaptic protein composition (reduced PSD95, GluR1, GluR2; increased SHANK3), hyperactivity, impulsivity, memory deficits, decreased prepulse inhibition, and a mania-like phenotype partially rescued by valproate treatment (PMID: 35368073).

**Consistency with human phenotype:** The heterozygous mouse phenotype (ventricular zone disruption, neuronal migration defects) is directly consistent with the subcortical heterotopia observed in the human patient (PMID: 35585091). The full knockout behavioral phenotypes (cognitive impairment, autistic-like behavior, hyperactivity) are broadly consistent with the ASD and neurodevelopmental phenotypes reported in human cases, though epileptic encephalopathy has not been specifically modeled or reported in any mouse line.

**Strength of model organism evidence: MODERATE**
The heterozygous mouse data provide meaningful support for haploinsufficiency as a pathogenic mechanism relevant to human neurodevelopmental disease. However, spontaneous seizures or an epileptic encephalopathy phenotype have not been documented in any mouse model, which is a significant gap given the specific disease context of this report.

## 4. BIOLOGICAL EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Known pathways and molecular function:** DLGAP4 is a core scaffold protein of the excitatory postsynaptic density (PSD), where it physically bridges PSD95 (via its N-terminal domain) and SHANK proteins (via its C-terminal domain), thereby organizing the PSD95–SAPAP–SHANK ternary complex that anchors and regulates AMPA and NMDA receptor trafficking (PMID: 28870203). Disruption of this complex — as demonstrated by altered GluR1, GluR2, PSD95, and SHANK3 levels in knockout mice (PMID: 35368073) — impairs glutamatergic synaptic transmission and plasticity. Additionally, DLGAP4 has a non-synaptic neurodevelopmental function: it regulates actin cytoskeleton dynamics and adherens junction integrity at the apical domain of radial glial progenitors, thereby controlling ventricular surface integrity and neuronal migration during cortical development (PMID: 35585091).

**Expression pattern:** The long DLGAP4 transcript is specifically expressed in fetal and adult cerebellum, with particularly high expression in Purkinje cells of both mouse and human brain (PMID: 24986922; ASHG 2010 – Minocherhomji et al.). DLGAP4 is also expressed in cortical progenitors during neurodevelopment (PMID: 35585091). This brain-enriched and developmentally regulated expression pattern strongly supports neurological relevance.

**Key experimental findings:**
- Functional rescue experiments demonstrated that wild-type, but not patient-derived mutant, DLGAP4 could rescue the ventricular surface and neuronal migration phenotype caused by Dlgap4 knockdown in mouse cortex, directly implicating the variant as loss-of-function (PMID: 35585091).
- The chromosomal translocation disrupting DLGAP4 causes monoallelic hypermethylation of the DLGAP4 promoter CpG island, leading to epigenetic silencing of the disrupted allele and dysregulated expression — an unusual epigenetic mechanism of haploinsufficiency (PMID: 24986922).
- CircDLGAP4, a circular RNA derived from the DLGAP4 locus, has been shown to exert neuroprotective effects in models of ischemic stroke (PMID: 29114076), Parkinson's disease (PMID: 31761328), and sevoflurane-induced neurotoxicity (PMID: 38000773), further underscoring the broad neurological relevance of this locus, though these findings relate to the non-coding circular RNA rather than the protein-coding gene directly.

**Strength of biological evidence: MODERATE**
The molecular function of DLGAP4 at the excitatory synapse is well-characterized, its brain-specific expression is established, and functional experiments directly link patient variants to loss of protein function. However, the specific biological mechanism connecting DLGAP4 haploinsufficiency to epileptic encephalopathy (as opposed to heterotopia, ataxia, or ASD) has not been elucidated.

---

## 5. OVERALL ASSESSMENT

**Overall evidence level: LIMITED**

**Key strengths:**
- Convergent evidence from three independent clinical reports, multiple mouse models, and robust molecular biology supports DLGAP4 as a bona fide neurodevelopmental disease gene.
- The heterozygous mouse knockout phenotype (ventricular zone disruption, neuronal migration defects) directly mirrors the subcortical heterotopia observed in the human patient, providing strong mechanistic support for autosomal dominant haploinsufficiency (PMID: 35585091).
- Functional rescue experiments with a patient-derived variant provide direct experimental evidence of variant pathogenicity (PMID: 35585091).
- The gene's role as a core PSD scaffold protein at glutamatergic synapses provides a biologically plausible mechanism for epilepsy susceptibility.

**Key gaps and limitations:**
- The total number of reported patients is very small (approximately 4 individuals), and no patient has been reported with a primary diagnosis of epileptic encephalopathy; epilepsy is only implied secondarily through the heterotopia phenotype.
- No recurrent heterozygous point loss-of-function or pathogenic missense variants have been identified; all reported variants are structural (chromosomal translocations) or uncharacterized.
- Spontaneous seizures or an epileptic encephalopathy phenotype have not been documented in any mouse model, and no electrophysiological studies demonstrating epileptiform activity have been reported.
- The phenotypic spectrum across cases (heterotopia, cerebellar ataxia, ASD) is broad and heterogeneous, making genotype–phenotype correlation difficult and raising the possibility that different mechanisms (epigenetic, structural, sequence-level) may produce distinct phenotypes.

**Conclusion:** DLGAP4 is a biologically compelling candidate neurodevelopmental disease gene with emerging clinical and experimental evidence supporting haploinsufficiency as a pathogenic mechanism. However, the current evidence base is insufficient to classify DLGAP4 as an established epileptic encephalopathy gene under a heterozygous autosomal dominant model; the gene should be considered a *limited/emerging candidate* for this specific indication. Identification of additional patients with heterozygous point LoF variants and documented epileptic encephalopathy, ideally through large-scale sequencing cohorts, is required to strengthen this association.
