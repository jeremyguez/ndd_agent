# Evidence Report: SOBP as a Neurodevelopmental Disorder Gene

---

## 1. GENE SUMMARY

SOBP (Sine Oculis Binding Protein) encodes a nuclear zinc finger protein containing FCS-type zinc finger domains and nuclear localization signals, originally identified as a binding partner of the transcription factor Six1. Its primary known functions include modulation of Six1/Eya1 transcriptional activity, cochlear development, and neurodevelopmental processes—particularly in the brain limbic system during synaptogenesis.

---

## 2. CLINICAL EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Number of independent case reports/series:** One published case series/report was identified (PMID: 21035105).

**Total approximate number of patients:** 2 patients from a single family.

**Most common phenotypes observed:** Intellectual disability (both syndromic and nonsyndromic forms within the same family), anterior maxillary protrusion, strabismus (collectively constituting MRAMS syndrome in the syndromic case), temporal lobe epilepsy, psychosis, and subclinical cochlear hearing loss (PMID: 21035105).

**Predominant variant type:** Loss-of-function (truncating mutation). The specific nucleotide/amino acid notation was not provided in the available abstract, but the variant was described as truncating and segregated with the phenotype under autosomal recessive inheritance (PMID: 21035105).

**Known cohort affiliation:** No affiliation with recognized large-scale cohorts (UDN, GREGoR, DDD, RUMC, or GEL) was reported.

**Critical note regarding inheritance:** The available clinical evidence supports **autosomal recessive** inheritance, not autosomal dominant (heterozygous) inheritance. The truncating mutation identified in PMID: 21035105 segregated recessively within the family, and the mouse model (jc/jc homozygous mutants) further supports a recessive mechanism. There is currently **no published clinical evidence** specifically supporting heterozygous (autosomal dominant) pathogenic variants in SOBP as a cause of neurodevelopmental disease.

**Strength of clinical evidence (for autosomal dominant/heterozygous disease): NONE**
**Strength of clinical evidence (for autosomal recessive disease): LIMITED** (single family, 2 patients, no independent replication identified in the available literature)

---

## 3. MODEL ORGANISM EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Organisms studied:** Mouse (*Mus musculus*, the naturally occurring *jc*/Jackson circler mutant) and *Xenopus laevis* (morpholino-based knockdown and overexpression).

**Key phenotypes:**

- In the *jc/jc* homozygous mouse mutant, cochlear duct growth arrests at embryonic day E13.5, with supernumerary hair cells, mirror-image duplications, and ectopic vestibular-like hair cells in the organ of Corti, establishing Sobp as critical for cochlear growth, cell fate determination, and patterning (PMID: 18579736). Proteomic analysis of *jc/jc* versus *+/jc* mouse brains identified 24 proteins with >1.5-fold expression differences, including dynamin and pacsin1, implicating SOBP in neuronal protein networks relevant to intellectual disability (PMID: 21035105).
- In *Xenopus*, morpholino-mediated knockdown of Sobp disrupted formation of ectodermal domains at neural plate stages, impaired otic vesicle development, and caused craniofacial cartilage defects. Overexpression produced similar disruptions, and expression of a construct carrying a human disease variant recapitulated pre-placodal ectoderm disruption with additional distinct changes (PMID: 34414417).

**Consistency with human phenotype:** The cochlear and craniofacial phenotypes in model organisms are broadly consistent with the human phenotype (hearing loss, craniofacial features in MRAMS syndrome). The neurological/intellectual disability phenotype is supported indirectly by proteomic brain data in mice and by limbic system expression, but direct neurological phenotyping of the mouse model is not prominently described in the available evidence. Importantly, all model organism studies employ homozygous loss or complete knockdown, consistent with recessive rather than dominant mechanisms.

**Strength of model organism evidence: MODERATE** (robust cochlear and craniofacial phenotypes in two vertebrate systems; neurological phenotype less directly characterized; all models reflect biallelic/complete loss of function)

---

## 4. BIOLOGICAL EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Known pathways and molecular function:** SOBP functions as a transcriptional co-factor in the Six1/Eya1 pathway. Co-immunoprecipitation and immunofluorescence experiments demonstrate that SOBP binds to and colocalizes with Six1 in the cell nucleus, and luciferase assays show that SOBP interferes with Six1+Eya1-mediated transcriptional activation, identifying it as a modulator—rather than a simple activator—of this pathway (PMID: 34414417). By contrast, the structurally related protein Zmym2 does not co-immunoprecipitate with Six1 and does not affect Six1+Eya1 activity, underscoring the specificity of SOBP's interaction (PMID: 40448369). The Six1/Eya1 pathway is well established in craniofacial, otic, and renal development, and is implicated in Branchio-oto-renal (BOR) syndrome.

**Expression pattern:** In situ RNA hybridization in postnatal mouse brain revealed strong SOBP expression in the limbic system during the period of active synaptogenesis—a pattern described as unusual among genes mutated in intellectual disability—suggesting a role in learning, memory, and affective behavior (PMID: 21035105). In mice, Jxc1/Sobp mRNA is also expressed in inner ear sensory hair cells, supporting cells, acoustic ganglia, developing retina, olfactory epithelium, trigeminal ganglion, and hair follicles, indicating broad expression across sensory organs during development (PMID: 18579736). In chickens, SOBP is expressed across many tissues throughout embryonic development, with higher expression in dorsal skin and feather follicles of certain breeds (PMID: 22802190).

**Key experimental findings:**
- SOBP encodes a nuclear zinc finger protein; wild-type protein localizes to the nucleus, while mutant isoforms are mislocalized to the cytoplasm, indicating that nuclear localization is functionally critical (PMID: 18579736).
- Proteomic analysis of *jc/jc* mouse brains implicates SOBP in neuronal endocytosis and synaptic vesicle recycling networks via dysregulation of dynamin and pacsin1 (PMID: 21035105).
- Intragenic exon rearrangements involving SOBP exons 2 and 3 (chromosomal locus 6q21) have been identified as tumor-specific lesions in non-Hodgkin B-cell lymphoma, and lower SOBP expression has been associated with inferior prognosis in ovarian carcinoma, suggesting broader roles beyond neurodevelopment (PMID: 31686349; PMID: 33742531). These oncological findings are not directly relevant to neurodevelopmental disease but indicate that SOBP disruption has functional consequences in multiple tissue contexts.

**Strength of biological evidence: MODERATE** (well-characterized molecular interaction with Six1, relevant expression in brain limbic system and sensory organs, functional nuclear localization data, and proteomic brain data; however, direct mechanistic studies linking SOBP to neuronal function or synaptic biology remain limited)

---

## 5. OVERALL ASSESSMENT

**Overall evidence level for heterozygous (autosomal dominant) SOBP as a neurodevelopmental disorder gene: INSUFFICIENT**

**Key strengths of the evidence:**
- A truncating SOBP variant has been directly linked to intellectual disability (including syndromic MRAMS syndrome and nonsyndromic ID with psychosis) in a human family, with functional support from the *jc/jc* mouse model (PMID: 21035105).
- Strong and specific expression of SOBP in the brain limbic system during synaptogenesis provides biologically plausible neurodevelopmental relevance (PMID: 21035105).
- Robust model organism data in both mouse and *Xenopus* confirm that loss of Sobp function causes developmental defects in tissues relevant to the human phenotype (PMID: 18579736; PMID: 34414417).
- A well-defined molecular mechanism—modulation of Six1/Eya1 transcriptional activity—provides a credible biological framework (PMID: 34414417).

**Key gaps and limitations:**
- The only published clinical evidence involves a single family with 2 patients and supports **autosomal recessive**, not autosomal dominant, inheritance. No heterozygous carriers in that family were reported to be affected (PMID: 21035105).
- There is a complete absence of published case reports, cohort studies, or variant databases entries specifically documenting heterozygous pathogenic SOBP variants causing neurodevelopmental disease.
- All model organism studies employ homozygous or complete-knockdown conditions; haploinsufficiency has not been demonstrated in any system.
- Independent replication of the original family finding has not been reported in the available literature.

**Conclusion:** The current published evidence establishes SOBP as a credible autosomal recessive intellectual disability gene with supporting functional and model organism data, but there is **no published evidence** to support heterozygous (autosomal dominant) pathogenic variants as a cause of neurodevelopmental disorder. Before SOBP can be considered a dominant neurodevelopmental disease gene, independent case identification—ideally through large-scale cohort studies or matchmaking platforms—and demonstration of haploinsufficiency in model systems would be required. The existing recessive evidence base is itself limited to a single family and warrants further replication.

1. SOBP is mutated in syndromic and nonsyndromic intellectual disability and is highly expressed in the brain limbic system (PMID: 21035105)

1. Sobp modulates the transcriptional activation of Six1 target genes and is required during craniofacial development (PMID: 34414417)
