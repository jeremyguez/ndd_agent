# Literature Evidence Report: SRSF1 as a Neurodevelopmental Disorder Gene

---

## 1. GENE SUMMARY

SRSF1 (Serine/Arginine-Rich Splicing Factor 1), located at chromosomal region 17q22, encodes a prototypical member of the SR protein family that functions as a master regulator of pre-mRNA splicing, modulating both constitutive and alternative splicing through recognition of exonic splicing enhancer sequences via its two RNA recognition motifs (RRMs) and a C-terminal arginine/serine-rich (RS) domain. Beyond splicing, SRSF1 participates in mRNA stability, nuclear export, translation, and—as recently described—RNA G-quadruplex unfolding (PMID: 38567732), positioning it as a pleiotropic post-transcriptional regulator with broad roles in cell growth, differentiation, and development.

---

## 2. CLINICAL EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Number of independent case series:** Two independent reports were identified. The primary peer-reviewed publication (PMID: 37071997) and a corroborating conference presentation at ASHG 2022 (ASHG 2022 – Garde et al.) represent overlapping but partially distinct cohorts assembled through international data-sharing efforts, including the Solve-RD consortium.

**Total approximate number of patients:** Approximately 17 patients are described in the published study (PMID: 37071997), with 16 patients reported at ASHG 2022. Given the overlapping international consortia, some patients may be shared across both reports; the combined non-redundant cohort is estimated at approximately 17–20 individuals.

**Most common phenotypes observed:** Across both reports, the core phenotypic spectrum of SRSF1 haploinsufficiency includes:
- Global developmental delay and intellectual disability (present in all or nearly all patients)
- Hypotonia
- Neurobehavioral problems / behavioral disorders (including features consistent with autism spectrum disorder)
- Skeletal anomalies (including variable marfanoid habitus, noted prominently in the ASHG 2022 report)
- Cardiac anomalies

**Predominant variant type:** Both loss-of-function (LoF) variants (frameshift and nonsense, totaling at least six distinct variants across the published cohort) and missense variants (seven identified in PMID: 37071997) were observed. Additionally, two microdeletions of the 17q22 region encompassing SRSF1 were reported, providing independent chromosomal-level evidence for haploinsufficiency. The majority of variants arose de novo. Pathogenicity of LoF variants and 5 of 7 missense variants was functionally validated using an in vivo Drosophila splicing assay, in silico structural modeling, and DNA methylation episignature analysis (PMID: 37071997). Germline mosaicism was suspected in at least one family (ASHG 2022 – Garde et al.).

**Known cohort affiliation:** Patients were recruited through international data-sharing platforms and the Solve-RD consortium. No specific affiliation with UDN, GREGoR, DDD, RUMC, or GEL cohorts was explicitly stated in the available evidence, though the multi-center international design is consistent with GeneMatcher-type collaborative ascertainment.

**Strength of clinical evidence: MODERATE**
The evidence is supported by a peer-reviewed publication with 17 patients, functional validation of variants, an established haploinsufficiency mechanism, and a consistent phenotypic spectrum across two independent reports. The cohort size remains modest, specific variant notations were not provided in the structured data, and independent replication in a fully separate cohort has not yet been documented in the peer-reviewed literature.

## 3. MODEL ORGANISM EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Organisms studied:** The model organism evidence is derived predominantly from mouse (*Mus musculus*) conditional knockout (cKO) studies across multiple tissue-specific contexts, with one rat model also reported. Drosophila melanogaster was used as a functional validation system in the clinical studies (ASHG 2022 – Garde et al.; PMID: 37071997).

**Key phenotypes from knockouts:**

- *Neurological/developmental relevance:* Direct neurological knockout phenotypes in mice are not reported in the available evidence. However, satellite cell-specific SRSF1 knockout (MyoD-Cre) caused early postnatal mortality and severe muscle atrophy with mitotic abnormalities and cellular senescence (PMID: 39381961), demonstrating that SRSF1 haploinsufficiency is not well-tolerated in rapidly proliferating progenitor cell populations—a finding broadly relevant to neurodevelopment. Regulatory T cell-specific knockout produced early lethal systemic autoimmunity with multi-organ inflammation (PMID: 36368121).

- *Lethality:* Two conditional knockout models resulted in perinatal or early postnatal lethality: the MyoD-Cre skeletal muscle model (PMID: 39381961) and the Treg-specific model (PMID: 36368121), underscoring the essential, non-redundant role of SRSF1 in multiple developmental contexts.

- *Infertility:* Two independent cKO models demonstrated infertility—male infertility due to failure of spermatogonial stem cell homing (PMID: 38271475) and female infertility due to impaired primordial follicle formation and failed meiotic synapsis (PMID: 36882745). These findings are consistent with the broad developmental roles of SRSF1 and may be relevant to the reproductive phenotype in affected individuals, though this was not specifically highlighted in the clinical reports.

- *Other tissue-specific phenotypes:* Pancreatic β-cell-specific knockout caused progressive glucose intolerance and impaired insulin secretion (PMID: 41138848); endothelial-specific knockout impaired ischemia-induced angiogenesis (PMID: 41195527); T cell-specific knockout exacerbated nephritis and altered iNKT cell development (PMID: 32810232; PMID: 34522020).

- *Drosophila:* Eye-specific overexpression of SF2 (the Drosophila SRSF1 ortholog) induced a severe developmental eye phenotype through missplicing of key developmental genes; human SRSF1 phenocopied this effect, confirming functional conservation across species (ASHG 2022 – Garde et al.). In vivo Drosophila splicing assays were used to validate the pathogenicity of patient-derived variants (PMID: 37071997).

**Consistency with human phenotype:** The mouse models do not include a germline heterozygous knockout that directly recapitulates the human neurodevelopmental syndrome, which is a notable gap. However, the consistent finding that SRSF1 loss is poorly tolerated across multiple progenitor and developmental cell types—including lethality in two cKO models—supports the biological plausibility of haploinsufficiency causing a multisystem developmental disorder. The skeletal muscle and cardiac phenotypes observed in cKO models are broadly consistent with the hypotonia and cardiac anomalies seen in patients.

**Strength of model organism evidence: MODERATE**
Multiple well-characterized conditional knockout mouse models demonstrate essential roles for SRSF1 in diverse developmental processes, and Drosophila studies directly validate patient variant pathogenicity. However, no germline heterozygous mouse model recapitulating the human neurodevelopmental phenotype has been reported, limiting direct translational inference.

## 4. BIOLOGICAL EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Known pathways and molecular function:** SRSF1 is a central node in multiple signaling and regulatory pathways. It functions downstream of the mTORC1-SRPK2 axis, where SRPK2-mediated phosphorylation of SRSF1 promotes its interaction with FAM120A to couple transcription and splicing of lipogenic genes (PMID: 37595559). SRSF1 modulates alternative splicing of ATF3 pre-mRNA to regulate the ATF3-KLF2-S1PR1 angiogenic pathway (PMID: 41195527). It also stimulates SUMOylation of spliceosomal proteins during active splicing, regulating spliceosome assembly efficiency (PMID: 29741121). A novel function in binding and unfolding RNA G-quadruplexes—alleviating their inhibitory effect on gene expression—has been biochemically characterized (PMID: 38567732).

**Expression pattern and neurodevelopmental relevance:** The available evidence does not include direct brain-specific expression data or neurodevelopmental transcriptomic studies for SRSF1. However, SRSF1 is broadly expressed and its role as a master splicing regulator is particularly relevant to the nervous system, where alternative splicing is exceptionally prevalent and developmentally regulated. The clinical phenotype of intellectual disability and neurobehavioral problems is consistent with disruption of a ubiquitous splicing factor during brain development.

**Key experimental findings:** SRSF1 has been characterized as a proto-oncogene in multiple cancer contexts. Copy number gain and mRNA overexpression of SRSF1 were associated with poor survival in small cell lung cancer (PMID: 27093186). SRSF1 overexpression is sufficient to induce pancreatitis and accelerate KRAS-driven pancreatic cancer via alternative splicing of IL1R1 and MAPK pathway activation (PMID: 37098965). FOXA1 directly regulates SRSF1 expression in prostate cancer, where SRSF1 promotes oncogenic splicing of FLNA exon 30 (PMID: 36170835). Depletion of SRSF1 in CD8+ T cells and tumor cells enhanced antitumor immune responses, with a small-molecule inhibitor (TN2008) demonstrating in vivo efficacy (PMID: 39837814). These oncological findings, while not directly relevant to the neurodevelopmental phenotype, confirm that SRSF1 dosage is tightly regulated and that both gain and loss of function have significant biological consequences.

**Strength of biological evidence: MODERATE**
SRSF1 has well-established molecular functions in splicing regulation, with multiple validated downstream pathways and targets. The Drosophila functional assays directly link patient variants to splicing dysregulation. However, direct mechanistic studies in neural or neurodevelopmental contexts are absent from the current evidence base, and the specific downstream splicing targets responsible for the human neurodevelopmental phenotype have not been identified.

---

## 5. OVERALL ASSESSMENT

**Overall evidence level: MODERATE**

**Key strengths:**
- A peer-reviewed publication (PMID: 37071997) describes 17 patients with a consistent neurodevelopmental phenotype caused by de novo SRSF1 variants, supported by multiple orthogonal functional validation approaches (Drosophila in vivo splicing assays, in silico structural modeling, DNA methylation episignature).
- The haploinsufficiency mechanism is supported by both LoF variants and microdeletions of 17q22 encompassing SRSF1, as well as by the consistent finding that SRSF1 loss is poorly tolerated across multiple developmental cell types in mouse models.
- Functional conservation between human SRSF1 and its Drosophila ortholog SF2 has been directly demonstrated, enabling in vivo variant validation.
- The clinical phenotype is internally consistent across two independent international reports.

**Key gaps and limitations:**
- The total patient cohort remains modest (~17–20 individuals), and specific variant-level data (HGVS notation) were not available in the structured evidence, limiting independent assessment of variant pathogenicity classifications.
- No germline heterozygous mouse model directly recapitulating the human neurodevelopmental syndrome has been reported; existing mouse models are all tissue-specific conditional knockouts.
- Brain-specific expression data and mechanistic studies in neural cell types are absent, leaving the precise neurodevelopmental mechanism undefined.
- The two clinical reports likely share overlapping patients through the same international consortium, reducing the effective number of truly independent replications.

**Conclusion:** The cumulative evidence supports SRSF1 haploinsufficiency as the cause of a recognizable autosomal dominant syndromic neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by intellectual disability, developmental delay, hypotonia, neurobehavioral problems, and variable skeletal and cardiac anomalies. The gene-disease relationship is biologically plausible given SRSF1's essential role as a master splicing regulator across multiple developmental contexts, and functional validation in Drosophila directly supports the pathogenicity of identified variants. Further independent patient cohort replication, germline heterozygous animal modeling, and mechanistic studies in neural tissues are needed to elevate the overall evidence to STRONG.
