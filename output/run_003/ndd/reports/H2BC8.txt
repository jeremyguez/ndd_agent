# Evidence Report: H2BC8 as a Neurodevelopmental Disorder Gene

---

## 1. GENE SUMMARY

H2BC8 (Histone H2B Type 1-C/E/F/G/I, also known as HIST1H2BE) encodes a replication-dependent histone H2B protein, a core component of the nucleosome that plays a fundamental role in chromatin packaging, DNA replication, transcriptional regulation, and epigenetic gene expression control. As a member of the histone H2B family, it participates in the dynamic regulation of chromatin structure that is critical for proper cellular differentiation and development.

## 4. BIOLOGICAL EVIDENCE SUMMARY

- **Known pathways and molecular function:** H2BC8 encodes a canonical histone H2B protein involved in nucleosome assembly, chromatin compaction, and epigenetic regulation of transcription. Histone H2B proteins broadly participate in DNA damage response, cell cycle progression, and developmental gene regulation.
- **Expression pattern (neurodevelopmental relevance):** No brain-specific or neurodevelopmental expression data were identified in the retrieved evidence. The two studies that mention H2BC8 focus on non-neurological contexts.
- **Key experimental findings:**
  - H2BC8 was identified as one of nine pyroptosis-related genes with dysregulated expression in papillary thyroid cancer (PTC), incorporated into a prognostic "PyroScore" model predicting thyroid cancer-free interval, with expression validated across six external datasets, 16 cell lines, and 20 clinical sample pairs (PMID: 38287330). Its dysregulated expression was associated with tumor immune microenvironment remodeling, but no mechanistic neurodevelopmental role was described.
  - H2BC8 was identified as one of eleven hub genes in peripheral blood mononuclear cells whose expression correlated with CPAP treatment response in obstructive sleep apnea patients, contributing to a random forest predictive model with an AUC of 0.92 (PMID: 34529670). Expression was partially correlated with monocyte infiltration associated with OSA remission, but again no neurodevelopmental mechanism was implicated.
- **Strength of biological evidence: LIMITED**

The available biological evidence is limited and entirely indirect with respect to neurodevelopmental disease. Both studies identify H2BC8 through computational/bioinformatic approaches (WGCNA, prognostic modeling) in non-neurological disease contexts. Neither study provides functional mechanistic data, brain expression profiling, or any experimental evidence linking H2BC8 to neurodevelopment or neurological function.

---

## 5. OVERALL ASSESSMENT

- **Overall evidence level: INSUFFICIENT**

**Key strengths of the evidence:**
- H2BC8 belongs to the histone H2B family, and other histone genes (e.g., those implicated in Wiedemann-Steiner syndrome, Rahman syndrome, and related chromatinopathies) have established roles in neurodevelopmental disorders, providing a plausible biological rationale for investigation.
- The gene's role in chromatin regulation is broadly relevant to developmental gene expression programs.

**Key gaps and limitations:**
- There are no clinical reports of patients with neurodevelopmental disorders harboring pathogenic variants in H2BC8.
- No model organism studies have examined the neurodevelopmental consequences of H2BC8 disruption.
- The only available biological evidence comes from bioinformatic studies in thyroid cancer and obstructive sleep apnea, neither of which addresses neurodevelopmental function or provides mechanistic insight relevant to a Mendelian disease hypothesis.
- No brain-specific expression data, protein interaction studies in neuronal contexts, or functional assays were identified.

**Conclusion:** At present, there is insufficient evidence to support H2BC8 as a neurodevelopmental disorder gene associated with heterozygous (autosomal dominant) pathogenic variants. While the gene's membership in the histone H2B family provides a theoretical basis for developmental relevance by analogy to related chromatinopathy genes, no clinical, model organism, or directly relevant biological evidence has been identified to substantiate this hypothesis. Systematic patient cohort screening, functional studies in neuronal models, and model organism characterization are needed before any disease association can be proposed.
