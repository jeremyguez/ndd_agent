# Evidence Report: ADCY5 as an Autism Spectrum Disorder Gene (Heterozygous / Autosomal Dominant)

---

## 1. GENE SUMMARY

ADCY5 (Adenylyl Cyclase 5) encodes a transmembrane adenylyl cyclase that catalyzes the conversion of ATP to cyclic AMP (cAMP), serving as a critical second messenger in intracellular signaling. It is the predominant adenylyl cyclase isoform expressed in medium spiny neurons (MSNs) of the striatum, where it integrates dopaminergic, cortical, and thalamic signaling to regulate voluntary movement initiation and suppress involuntary movements (PMID: 33934385).

---

## 2. CLINICAL EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Number of independent reports:** Two reports from the structured evidence base directly address ADCY5 and autism spectrum disorder (ASD): one conference abstract from ASHG 2013 and one from ASHG 2014. No peer-reviewed, published case series or case reports specifically establishing heterozygous ADCY5 variants as a cause of ASD were identified.

**Total approximate number of patients:** Across the two ASD-relevant reports, the evidence is extremely sparse. The ASHG 2013 abstract (ASHG 2013 - Game of Exomes and Autism Spectrum Disorder) reported a gene-based association analysis (SKAT-O p=0.002) in an ASD cohort, noting two prior de novo mutations in ADCY5, but did not report individually confirmed pathogenic cases. The ASHG 2014 abstract (ASHG 2014 - Diagnostic exome sequencing of patients with Autism Spectrum Disorder) described a single patient with an inherited (non-de novo) ADCY5 variant classified as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS), in the context of a broader ASD cohort with additional features including seizures, intellectual disability, or dysmorphic features.

**Most common phenotypes observed:** In the ASD-specific reports, the phenotype is simply ASD, with no consistent additional features attributable specifically to ADCY5. It is worth noting that the well-established ADCY5 phenotype across the broader literature (PMID: 27061943; PMID: 31538084; PMID: 33934385) is a hyperkinetic movement disorder — chorea, dystonia, myoclonus, and paroxysmal exacerbations — not ASD. One mouse knockout study did demonstrate autistic-like behaviors (PMID: 27878759), but this involved loss-of-function in the dorsal striatum, a mechanism distinct from the gain-of-function heterozygous variants established in human disease.

**Predominant variant type:** The ASHG 2013 report involved rare variants of unspecified type identified by gene-based burden analysis. The ASHG 2014 report involved a single inherited variant of uncertain significance. Neither report specifies missense, loss-of-function, or de novo status in a manner sufficient to define a pathogenic mechanism for ASD.

**Known cohorts:** Neither report derives from established rare disease cohorts such as UDN, GREGoR, DDD, RUMC, or GEL. The ASHG 2013 data originated from the University of Miami / HIHG, and the ASHG 2014 data from Ambry Genetics (Aliso Viejo, CA). Both are conference abstracts, not peer-reviewed publications.

**Strength of clinical evidence: NONE**
There are no published, peer-reviewed case reports or case series establishing heterozygous ADCY5 variants as a cause of ASD. The available data consist solely of two conference abstracts with nominally significant or uncertain findings, no confirmed de novo pathogenic variants in ASD probands, and no replication in independent cohorts.

---

## 3. MODEL ORGANISM EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Organisms studied:** Mouse (*Mus musculus*) and zebrafish (*Danio rerio*).

**Key phenotypes relevant to ASD:** One mouse study is directly relevant. Full knockout of *Adcy5* in mice produced increased repetitive behaviors and sociability deficits — core features of ASD — which were rescued by blocking mGluR5 glutamate receptors in the dorsal striatum (PMID: 27878759). Targeted siRNA-mediated suppression of AC5 specifically in the dorsal striatum replicated these autistic-like behavioral phenotypes, implicating striatal cAMP signaling in social and repetitive behavior regulation. Importantly, this model involves complete loss of function, not the heterozygous gain-of-function mechanism established in human ADCY5-related dyskinesia.

A separate knock-in mouse model carrying a gain-of-function ADCY5 variant recapitulated hyperkinetic movement disorder with increased striatal cAMP signaling, responsive to caffeine via adenosine A2A receptor blockade (PMID: 41660259), consistent with the human movement disorder phenotype but not with ASD.

**Consistency with human ASD phenotype:** The mouse knockout data provide biological plausibility for a role of ADCY5 in striatal circuits relevant to repetitive and social behaviors. However, the mechanism (complete loss of function) is inconsistent with the heterozygous gain-of-function variants that cause human ADCY5-related dyskinesia, and no human ASD cases with confirmed loss-of-function ADCY5 variants have been reported. The zebrafish models addressed pigmentation and melanocyte biology (PMID: 36430661), with no neurological or behavioral relevance to ASD.

**Strength of model organism evidence: LIMITED**
There is one mouse study demonstrating ASD-relevant behavioral phenotypes upon *Adcy5* knockout, but the mechanistic direction (loss-of-function) is opposite to the established human disease mechanism (gain-of-function), and no model directly recapitulates heterozygous dominant ASD-associated variants.

## 4. BIOLOGICAL EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Known pathways and molecular function:** ADCY5 catalyzes cAMP synthesis downstream of G-protein-coupled receptors, integrating dopaminergic (D1/D2), adenosinergic (A2A), and other GPCR signals in striatal MSNs (PMID: 33934385). Gain-of-function mutations associated with dyskinesia cluster at the AC5–Gβγ interface, reducing conditional Gβγ-mediated regulation and resulting in constitutively elevated cAMP (PMID: 38589608). Elevated cAMP in striatal neurons is the established pathogenic mechanism for the movement disorder. In pancreatic β-cells, ADCY5 is indispensable for glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (PMID: 24740569), and common regulatory variants at the ADCY5 locus are associated with fasting glucose and type 2 diabetes risk (ASHG 2015 - A functional regulatory variant associated with type 2 diabetes and fasting glucose levels at the ADCY5 locus).

**Expression pattern:** ADCY5 is most highly expressed in striatal MSNs, where it is the dominant adenylyl cyclase isoform (PMID: 33934385). This expression pattern is highly relevant to movement control and, given the role of striatal circuits in reward, habit, and social behavior, provides indirect biological plausibility for neurodevelopmental phenotypes. However, no specific neurodevelopmental or ASD-focused expression studies were identified in the evidence base.

**Key experimental findings relevant to ASD:** The cell-based study demonstrating that ADCY5 gain-of-function (R418W) increases cAMP production, and that this is suppressed by caffeine, theophylline, and istradefylline (PMID: 36867608), is mechanistically important for the movement disorder but does not address ASD biology. The mouse knockout study linking striatal AC5 loss to mGluR5 upregulation and autistic-like behavior (PMID: 27878759) is the only experimental finding with direct ASD relevance, but it involves a loss-of-function mechanism.

**Strength of biological evidence: LIMITED**
ADCY5 has a well-characterized role in striatal cAMP signaling with indirect relevance to neurodevelopmental circuits, but no direct experimental evidence links heterozygous gain-of-function ADCY5 variants to ASD-relevant molecular or cellular phenotypes.

---

## 5. OVERALL ASSESSMENT

**Overall evidence level: INSUFFICIENT**

**Key strengths:**
- ADCY5 is biologically plausible as a contributor to striatal circuit dysfunction, given its dominant expression in MSNs and its role in dopaminergic signal integration.
- One mouse knockout study demonstrated ASD-relevant behavioral phenotypes (repetitive behaviors, sociability deficits), providing proof-of-concept for a striatal cAMP–ASD connection (PMID: 27878759).
- A nominally significant gene-based association with ASD was reported in one conference abstract (ASHG 2013), suggesting the gene may warrant further investigation.

**Key gaps and limitations:**
- No peer-reviewed, published case reports or case series confirm heterozygous ADCY5 variants as a cause of ASD in humans.
- The only ASD-relevant human genetic data are from two unpublished conference abstracts, one of which identified only a VUS inherited variant (not de novo), and neither of which has been replicated.
- The established human disease mechanism for ADCY5 is heterozygous gain-of-function causing a hyperkinetic movement disorder, not ASD; the mouse ASD model used complete loss-of-function, representing a mechanistically discordant model.
- No patients from established rare disease discovery cohorts (UDN, GREGoR, DDD, GEL) with ADCY5 variants and ASD phenotypes were identified.

**Conclusion:** The current evidence is insufficient to support ADCY5 as an ASD gene with heterozygous autosomal dominant pathogenic variants. While ADCY5 has a well-established role in striatal neurobiology and gain-of-function variants cause a distinct hyperkinetic movement disorder, the ASD-specific evidence is limited to two conference abstracts and a mechanistically discordant mouse model. Rigorous prospective studies with confirmed de novo or dominant pathogenic variants in ASD probands, ideally within established rare disease cohorts, would be required before any clinical classification could be considered.
