# STXBP1 as an Autism Spectrum Disorder Gene: Literature Evidence Report

---

## 1. GENE SUMMARY

STXBP1 (Syntaxin Binding Protein 1, also known as Munc18-1) encodes a highly conserved presynaptic protein that is essential for SNARE complex assembly and synaptic vesicle exocytosis. Beyond its canonical synaptic role, STXBP1 also participates in non-synaptic functions including neuronal cytoskeletal organization, membrane protein trafficking, and suppression of α-synuclein aggregation (PMID: 41510908; PMID: 31221716).

---

## 2. CLINICAL EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Number of independent studies and patients:**
Ten independent clinical reports or series were identified. Across the larger cohort studies alone, the total number of patients exceeds 930 individuals: 534 (PMID: 35190816), 162 (PMID: 38015929), 147 (PMID: 26865513), 48 (PMID: 35655584), and 40 (PMID: 39392525), supplemented by several single case reports and small series from conference abstracts (ASHG 2016, 2017, 2018).

**Most common phenotypes observed:**
The dominant clinical presentation across all studies is a developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (DEE). Core features include:
- **Epilepsy** (present in ~95% of patients; PMID: 26865513), with seizure types including infantile/epileptic spasms, focal-onset seizures, tonic seizures, and West syndrome or Ohtahara syndrome
- **Intellectual disability**, ranging from mild to profound (severe-to-profound in ~88%; PMID: 26865513)
- **Neurodevelopmental delay**, affecting gross motor and expressive communication domains (PMID: 38015929)
- **Autism spectrum disorder (ASD)** and autistic features, reported across multiple studies (PMID: 26865513; PMID: 39359953; ASHG 2016)
- **Movement disorders** and extrapyramidal features (PMID: 26865513)
- **SUDEP and early mortality**, with a median age of death of 13 years in a dedicated mortality cohort (PMID: 39392525)

Regarding ASD specifically: autistic features are consistently noted as part of the STXBP1 phenotypic spectrum across cohort studies (PMID: 26865513), and at least one case report describes a novel splice-site variant (c.38-2A>G) in a child presenting with intellectual disability, early-onset seizures, and autism spectrum disorder — described as the first STXBP1 splice-site variant associated with ASD (PMID: 39359953). An ASHG 2016 abstract also reported ASD in a patient with a de novo 4.9 kb deletion of STXBP1 exons 3–4 (ASHG 2016 - Uddin et al.). Additionally, a case of STXBP1 p.N548I presented with hand flapping and attention deficit features consistent with ASD (ASHG 2017 - Pappas et al.).

**Predominant variant type:**
Both loss-of-function (LoF) variants (protein-truncating variants, splice-site variants, and whole-gene or exonic deletions) and missense variants are well-represented. In the largest cohort (PMID: 35190816), 261 of 534 individuals carried LoF variants, while recurrent missense variants at hotspot residues (p.Arg406, p.Arg292, p.Arg551) accounted for the majority of the remaining cases. All confirmed pathogenic variants are heterozygous and de novo, consistent with an autosomal dominant mechanism driven by haploinsufficiency or dominant-negative effects.

**Known cohort membership:**
The studies draw from international multicenter collaborations and registries; no patients are explicitly identified as belonging to named cohorts such as UDN, GREGoR, DDD, RUMC, or GEL, though the scale and international scope of the largest studies (PMID: 35190816; PMID: 38015929) suggest overlap with such programs is likely.

**Strength of clinical evidence: STRONG**
The evidence base includes hundreds of independently ascertained patients with confirmed de novo heterozygous pathogenic variants, consistent phenotypic profiles across multiple international cohorts, and ACMG-classified pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants. ASD is a recognized, recurrently reported feature of the syndrome, though it is not the primary presenting phenotype.

## 3. MODEL ORGANISM EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Organisms studied:**
Mouse (*Mus musculus*), zebrafish (*Danio rerio*), *Drosophila melanogaster*, and *C. elegans* have all been used to model STXBP1-related disorders.

**Key phenotypes from knockouts:**

*Mouse:* Heterozygous constitutional knockout mice (*Stxbp1*+/−) are viable and recapitulate core features of the human disorder, including spontaneous seizures, spike-wave discharges, myoclonic jerks, cortical hyperexcitability, cognitive dysfunction, hyperactivity, and anxiety-like behavior (PMID: 29538625; PMID: 32073399). Mechanistically, haploinsufficiency impairs GABAergic synaptic transmission from parvalbumin- and somatostatin-expressing interneurons (PMID: 32073399), and disrupts excitatory recruitment of inhibitory interneurons in cortical microcircuits (PMID: 38086378). Absence seizures with spike-and-wave discharges arise specifically from impaired cortico-striatal excitatory transmission to fast-spiking interneurons (PMID: 31015467). Heterozygous mice also display enhanced aggressiveness and impaired fear learning, behavioral features relevant to ASD and psychiatric comorbidities (PMID: 29040524). GABAergic neuron-specific heterozygous mice show ~50% perinatal lethality, underscoring the critical role of STXBP1 in inhibitory neurons (PMID: 29538625). A humanized knock-in mouse model showed sub-Mendelian viability, particularly in males, with reduced STXBP1 protein abundance (PMID: 39229131). Conditional sparse knockout in forebrain neurons caused cell-autonomous neuronal death, rescued by wild-type but not fully by pathogenic STXBP1 mutants, which instead impaired dendritic growth (PMID: 41510908).

*Zebrafish:* Homozygous *stxbp1b* knockout larvae exhibit spontaneous electrographic seizures and reduced locomotor responses, providing a tractable seizure model; clemizole and trazodone reduced ictal-like event frequency by ~80–83%, while standard antiseizure medications were ineffective (PMID: 35451230; PMID: 26963117).

*C. elegans:* Humanized *unc-18* knockin worms expressing human STXBP1 rescued locomotion and electrophysiology defects of *unc-18* null animals; known pathogenic variants (R292H, R406H, R388X) installed at the humanized locus produced quantifiable functional deficits, with R388X showing a dominant-negative effect ~3-fold more severe than heterozygous knockout (ASHG 2019 - Humanized STXBP1 animal models).

*Drosophila:* Haploinsufficiency of Rop (the fly STXBP1 ortholog) in α-synuclein-transgenic flies increased insoluble α-synuclein aggregates, reduced dopamine neuron numbers, and impaired locomotion, linking STXBP1 to synucleinopathy pathways (ASHG 2023 - α-Synuclein pathology in Drosophila).

**Consistency with human phenotype:**
The mouse and zebrafish models closely recapitulate the epileptic, cognitive, and behavioral features of human STXBP1-related DEE. Behavioral phenotypes in mice (aggression, fear-learning deficits, anxiety) are consistent with the ASD and psychiatric features observed clinically.

**Strength of model organism evidence: STRONG**
Multiple independent laboratories across multiple species have reproduced neurological, epileptic, and neurobehavioral phenotypes consistent with the human disorder, using heterozygous models that mirror the human haploinsufficient genotype.

## 4. BIOLOGICAL EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Known pathways and molecular function:**
STXBP1 is a central regulator of SNARE-mediated synaptic vesicle exocytosis, binding syntaxin-1 and facilitating SNARE complex assembly required for neurotransmitter release (PMID: 31221716). Beyond this canonical presynaptic role, STXBP1 interacts with the neuronal cytoskeleton through α-II Spectrin and ARPC2, regulating membrane periodic structures and protein trafficking in a non-synaptic capacity (PMID: 41510908). STXBP1 also forms a tripartite complex with CASK and APBA1 during insulin secretion, demonstrating functional roles outside the central nervous system (PMID: 33159991). Network analyses further identify STXBP1 as a downstream target regulated by CASK and Cacnb1 in the context of presynaptic vesicle release (PMID: 40502014).

**Expression pattern:**
STXBP1 is developmentally regulated in the cerebral cortex and is detected in both synaptic and cytosolic neuronal fractions (PMID: 41510908). Its expression is critical during early neurodevelopment, as complete knockout causes widespread perinatal neurodegeneration in mice (PMID: 31221716).

**Key experimental findings:**
- Patient-derived iPSC GABAergic neurons carrying STXBP1 pathogenic variants showed dysfunctional electrophysiological maturation (reduced spontaneous spikes and bursts by MEA) and dysregulation of epilepsy- and neurodevelopment-associated genes including *KCNH1*, *KCNH5*, *RASGRF1*, and *SEMA3A*, despite only ~20% reduction in STXBP1 protein (PMID: 33961044). This supports GABAergic dysfunction as a primary pathogenic mechanism.
- A homozygous disease variant (L446F) produced a gain-of-function in synaptic transmission (2-fold increase in evoked release), demonstrating that STXBP1 mutations can produce divergent cellular mechanisms while converging on an encephalopathic phenotype (PMID: 31855252).
- STXBP1 haploinsufficiency depletes synaptic Doc2A and Doc2B, proteins involved in asynchronous neurotransmitter release, and disease-causing mutants cause Doc2 aggregation, identifying a downstream synaptic mechanism (PMID: 38242640).
- HSV-1 LAT-encoded miRNAs (miR-H3, miR-H4) target the 3'UTR of STXBP1 and reduce its expression, identifying STXBP1 as a component of the GABAergic pathway modulated during viral latency (PMID: 37668872).
- STXBP1 suppresses α-synuclein self-replicating aggregation, suggesting a molecular chaperone function that may link STXBP1 encephalopathy to synucleinopathies (PMID: 31221716; ASHG 2023).

**Strength of biological evidence: STRONG**
Converging evidence from patient iPSC models, mouse neuronal cultures, protein interaction studies, and cross-species functional assays robustly supports STXBP1's role in synaptic vesicle release, GABAergic circuit function, and neurodevelopmental gene regulation, all directly relevant to the observed human phenotype.

---

## 5. OVERALL ASSESSMENT

**Overall evidence level: STRONG**

**Key strengths:**
1. *Scale and replication of clinical evidence:* Over 930 patients with confirmed de novo heterozygous pathogenic STXBP1 variants have been reported across multiple independent international cohorts, with consistent phenotypic profiles. ASD and autistic features are a recognized component of the syndrome, documented in cohort studies and individual case reports.
2. *Cross-species model organism convergence:* Heterozygous mouse models, zebrafish knockouts, humanized *C. elegans*, and *Drosophila* haploinsufficiency models all recapitulate neurological and neurobehavioral features of the human disorder, including seizures, cognitive impairment, and ASD-relevant behavioral phenotypes.
3. *Mechanistic depth:* The molecular basis of pathogenicity is well-characterized at multiple levels — from SNARE complex assembly and GABAergic interneuron dysfunction to cytoskeletal regulation and downstream synaptic protein stability — providing strong biological plausibility for the observed neurodevelopmental phenotypes.

**Key gaps or limitations:**
- ASD is a secondary or comorbid feature within a broader DEE phenotype; STXBP1 has not been systematically studied as a primary ASD gene in ASD-only cohorts without epilepsy or intellectual disability.
- The frequency and severity of ASD specifically (as opposed to "autistic features") are not consistently quantified across the large cohort studies, limiting precise prevalence estimates.
- Most clinical data derive from epilepsy-focused registries, potentially underascertaining individuals with milder neurodevelopmental presentations including ASD without prominent seizures.

**Conclusion:**
STXBP1 is one of the most robustly supported genes in the developmental and epileptic encephalopathy landscape, with strong clinical, model organism, and biological evidence for autosomal dominant pathogenicity via heterozygous de novo variants. Autism spectrum disorder and autistic features are consistently reported as part of the STXBP1 phenotypic spectrum across multiple independent cohorts and case reports, supported by GABAergic dysfunction and synaptic dysregulation mechanisms directly relevant to ASD pathophysiology. However, STXBP1 should be classified as an ASD-associated gene in the context of a broader neurodevelopmental syndrome rather than as a primary isolated ASD gene, and dedicated ascertainment in ASD cohorts would strengthen this specific evidence base.
