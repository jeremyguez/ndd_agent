# Evidence Report: SYT1 as an Autism Spectrum Disorder / Neurodevelopmental Disorder Gene (Autosomal Dominant, Heterozygous)

---

## 1. GENE SUMMARY

SYT1 (Synaptotagmin-1, MIM #600216) encodes a synaptic vesicle membrane protein that functions as the primary calcium sensor mediating fast, synchronous neurotransmitter release during synaptic vesicle exocytosis. It achieves this through its tandem C2 domains (C2A and C2B), which interact with SNARE complexes (synaptobrevin-2, syntaxin-1, SNAP-25) and anionic phospholipids in a Ca²⁺-dependent manner, and it additionally clamps spontaneous and asynchronous release between action potentials (PMID: 36291375; PMID: 24692137).

---

## 2. CLINICAL EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Number of independent reports and total patients:**
Eight independent publications were identified, comprising six case series or cohort studies and two single case reports. Across these studies, the total number of individually described patients is approximately 79–80, spanning the original 11-patient series (PMID: 30107533), an expanded 22-patient series (PMID: 35101335), a 40-patient UK-based cohort (PMID: 41332143), a functional-correlation study reporting 8 specific variants in individual patients (PMID: 39481209), a 3-patient mechanistic study (PMID: 32362337), and three single case reports from Spain (PMID: 39049490), Brazil (PMID: 38283597), and an unspecified center (PMID: 38058756). An additional conference abstract (ASHG 2012 – Matsumoto et al.) described one patient with a large chromosomal deletion encompassing SYT1, though this is not a point-mutation case and is not counted in the missense total.

**Most common phenotypes observed:**
Across all missense-variant studies, a highly consistent core phenotype has emerged — now designated Baker-Gordon syndrome (BAGOS, OMIM #618218). The cardinal features include:
- Infantile hypotonia (often resolving with age)
- Moderate-to-profound intellectual disability / developmental delay
- Childhood-onset hyperkinetic movement disorder (worsening with age)
- Motor stereotypies
- Sleep disturbances (worsening with age)
- Episodic agitation / mood instability
- Congenital or early-onset ophthalmic abnormalities / visual impairments
- EEG abnormalities (reported in up to 93% of patients in the largest cohort; PMID: 41332143), though frank epileptic seizures are typically absent or uncommon
- Communication and social-communicative impairments (progressive)
- Self-injurious behaviors (worsening with age)

Less consistently reported features include MRI abnormalities (45% in PMID: 41332143), scoliosis, sialorrhea, respiratory problems, and mild dysmorphic features. One case also presented with mitochondrial respiratory chain deficiencies and rod-cone dysfunction (PMID: 39049490). Notably, the phenotype shows age-related evolution, with hypotonia predominating in infancy and movement disorder, sleep difficulties, and behavioral problems becoming more prominent in later childhood (PMID: 41332143).

**Predominant variant type:**
All pathogenic variants identified to date are heterozygous de novo missense variants. No loss-of-function (nonsense, frameshift, or splice-site) variants have been reported as causative in this disorder. Variants cluster predominantly in the C2B domain, with a smaller number in the C2A domain (PMID: 35101335). Specific recurrent or representative variants include p.Met303Lys, p.Asp304Gly, p.Asp366Glu, p.Ile368Thr, p.Asn371Lys (PMID: 30107533), p.Asp310Glu (PMID: 39049490), p.Ile368Thr (PMID: 38283597), and p.Leu159Arg, p.Thr196Lys, p.Glu209Lys, p.Glu219Gln, p.Met303Val, p.Ser309Pro, p.Tyr365Cys, p.Gly369Asp (PMID: 39481209). The mechanism of pathogenicity is dominant-negative rather than haploinsufficiency, as demonstrated by functional studies showing that mutant SYT1 protein impairs evoked exocytosis even in the presence of wild-type protein (PMID: 32362337; PMID: 39481209).

**Cohort affiliation:**
The largest cohort (40 patients; PMID: 41332143) was conducted under the UK-based Brain and Behavior in Neurodevelopmental Disorders of Genetic Origin (BBND) project. No patients were explicitly identified from the UDN, GREGoR, DDD, RUMC, or GEL cohorts in the available abstracts, though the multinational nature of several studies (PMID: 35101335; PMID: 39481209) suggests overlap with large diagnostic programs is possible.

**Regarding ASD specifically:**
While Baker-Gordon syndrome is classified as a neurodevelopmental disorder with intellectual disability, movement disorder, and behavioral disturbances, formal ASD diagnoses are not consistently reported across the clinical series. However, social-communicative impairments, motor stereotypies, episodic agitation, and behavioral disturbances — core features of the syndrome — overlap substantially with ASD symptomatology. An ASHG 2023 conference abstract (ASHG 2023 – Siddappa Niranjana Murthy et al.) identified SYT1 as differentially expressed in ASD post-mortem brain tissue and carrying deleterious variation in in-house WES data from Indian ASD children (n=15), providing preliminary genetic evidence for a link to ASD specifically, though this remains to be replicated in larger cohorts.

**Strength of clinical evidence: STRONG**
The evidence base includes multiple independent case series totaling approximately 79 patients, consistent core phenotype across international centers, exclusively de novo heterozygous missense variants, and functional validation in multiple studies.

## 3. MODEL ORGANISM EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Organisms studied:**
All model organism studies identified used the mouse (*Mus musculus*), employing heterozygous knockout, homozygous (full) knockout, conditional knockout, and knockdown approaches.

**Key phenotypes from knockouts:**
- *Heterozygous knockout (Syt1+/−)*: Mice were viable and showed reduced Syt1-positive cell counts in prelimbic cortex, hippocampus, amygdala, accumbens nucleus, caudoputamen, and ventral tegmental area. Behaviorally, they exhibited reduced locomotion, increased anxiety-like behavior (preference for peripheral/closed environments, reduced open-arm exploration in elevated plus maze), increased immobility in forced swim test, and elevated glutamate in the amygdala (PMID: 36031564). These findings are relevant to the human phenotype of behavioral disturbances, mood instability, and episodic agitation.
- *Full knockout (Syt1−/−)*: Used primarily as a background for mechanistic rescue experiments. Full knockout abolishes synchronous neurotransmitter release clamping, increases spontaneous and asynchronous (Syt7-mediated) release, and impairs readily releasable vesicle pool recovery under repetitive stimulation (PMID: 32015138; PMID: 20926654). Full knockout mice are not reported as viable to adulthood in most studies, consistent with the essential role of Syt1 in synaptic transmission.
- *Conditional knockout (retina-specific)*: Deletion of Syt1 from rods and/or cones abolished ERG b-waves and eliminated photoreceptor-driven responses in downstream retinal neurons (PMID: 36280223; PMID: 37426783; PMID: 40425313). This is directly relevant to the ophthalmic abnormalities and rod-cone dysfunction observed in human patients (PMID: 39049490).
- *Inhibitory synapse studies*: Combined conditional knockout of Syt1 and Syt2 at parvalbumin-expressing inhibitory synapses caused significant reduction and desynchronization of fast inhibitory release, while Syt1 knockout alone had modest effects at these synapses, indicating functional redundancy with Syt2 at specific synapse types (PMID: 28363983).

**Consistency with human phenotype:**
The mouse heterozygous knockout phenotype (anxiety, altered locomotion, behavioral abnormalities, reduced Syt1 expression in limbic and cortical regions) is broadly consistent with the human neurodevelopmental and behavioral phenotype. The retinal conditional knockout phenotype directly mirrors the ophthalmic findings in patients. The dominant-negative mechanism in humans (rather than simple haploinsufficiency) means that heterozygous knockout mice may underestimate the severity of the human condition, but the directional consistency is clear.

**Strength of model organism evidence: MODERATE**
Multiple independent mouse studies demonstrate neurological and behavioral phenotypes consistent with the human disorder, and retinal findings directly parallel patient ophthalmic features. However, no mouse model carrying the specific human pathogenic missense variants (dominant-negative) has been reported in the available literature, which limits direct mechanistic translation.

## 4. BIOLOGICAL EVIDENCE SUMMARY

**Known pathways and molecular function:**
SYT1 is a core component of the synaptic vesicle exocytosis machinery. Its C2A and C2B domains bind Ca²⁺, phospholipids, and the SNARE complex (synaptobrevin-2/VAMP2, syntaxin-1, SNAP-25) to trigger fast synchronous neurotransmitter release. Mechanistic studies have dissected the C2B-SNARE primary interface into functionally distinct regions: region II (binding SNAP-25) is essential for Ca²⁺-evoked release, while region I mediates vesicle priming and clamping of spontaneous release (PMID: 39374398). SYT1 also serves as a primary Zn²⁺ sensor under pathological conditions, with elevated intracellular Zn²⁺ increasing spontaneous release frequency via binding at the interface between the tandem C2 domains (PMID: 40753258). SYT1 interacts with SYT13 and SNARE complexes to regulate extracellular vesicle release, and its dysregulation has been implicated in synucleinopathies (PMID: 40551252). SYT1 also interacts with presenilin-1 to modulate amyloid-β production, with reduced SYT1 levels found in sporadic Alzheimer's disease brain tissue (PMID: 28193235).

**Expression pattern:**
SYT1 is broadly expressed in neurons throughout the brain, with particularly high expression in cortex, hippocampus, amygdala, accumbens nucleus, caudoputamen, and ventral tegmental area — regions critical for cognition, movement, and behavior (PMID: 36031564). It is also expressed in retinal photoreceptors (rods and cones), consistent with the ophthalmic phenotype in patients. A novel expression site in olfactory sensory neuron cilia has been identified, where Syt1 may participate in odorant receptor trafficking (PMID: 41125435). SYT1 was identified as differentially expressed in ASD post-mortem brain tissue (ASHG 2023 – Siddappa Niranjana Murthy et al.) and as one of seven key AD-associated palmitoylation-related genes in transcriptomic analyses of multiple GEO datasets (PMID: 41364313).

**Key experimental findings supporting pathogenicity:**
- Functional studies in mouse primary hippocampal cultures demonstrated that patient-derived missense variants impair SYT1 localization and synaptic vesicle kinetics in a dominant-negative manner (PMID: 30107533).
- Electrophysiological studies in cultured hippocampal neurons showed that pathogenic SYT1 variants cause dominant-negative impairment of evoked exocytosis, with the degree of impairment correlating with clinical severity (PMID: 39481209; PMID: 32362337).
- Molecular dynamics simulations and ACMG/AMP variant classification supported pathogenicity of 15 distinct variants (PMID: 35101335).
- Biophysical characterization of patient variants confirmed disruption of Ca²⁺-dependent membrane binding and SNARE interactions (PMID: 32362337).

**Strength of biological evidence: STRONG**
SYT1 has a well-characterized, essential molecular function in synaptic vesicle exocytosis. Multiple independent functional studies directly demonstrate that patient-derived variants impair this function through a dominant-negative mechanism, with genotype-phenotype correlations established.

---

## 5. OVERALL ASSESSMENT

**Overall evidence level: STRONG**

**Key strengths:**
1. *Robust clinical evidence*: Approximately 79 patients across eight independent publications from multiple international centers, all carrying de novo heterozygous missense variants with a highly consistent and recognizable core phenotype (Baker-Gordon syndrome). The disorder has received an OMIM designation (#618218).
2. *Mechanistic coherence*: The dominant-negative pathogenic mechanism is directly supported by multiple independent functional studies in primary neurons, with genotype-function-phenotype correlations established (PMID: 39481209; PMID: 32362337; PMID: 30107533). All variants cluster in functionally critical C2 domains.
3. *Model organism support*: Heterozygous mouse knockouts recapitulate behavioral and neurological features relevant to the human phenotype, and retinal conditional knockouts directly mirror patient ophthalmic findings (PMID: 36031564; PMID: 36280223).

**Key gaps and limitations:**
1. *ASD-specific evidence is limited*: The primary clinical entity is Baker-Gordon syndrome, a broader neurodevelopmental disorder. Formal ASD diagnoses are not systematically reported in the clinical series, and the only direct ASD-specific genetic evidence comes from a small conference abstract (n=15 WES cases; ASHG 2023 – Siddappa Niranjana Murthy et al.) that has not yet been published as a peer-reviewed study.
2. *No knock-in mouse models*: No published mouse model carrying specific human pathogenic missense variants (dominant-negative) has been reported, limiting in vivo validation of the dominant-negative mechanism.
3. *Variant spectrum*: All reported pathogenic variants are missense; the absence of LoF variants as a disease mechanism means standard haploinsufficiency-based evidence frameworks (e.g., pLI scores) may not fully apply, and the dominant-negative mechanism requires individual functional validation for each new variant.

**Conclusion:**
SYT1 is a well-established neurodevelopmental disorder gene in which de novo heterozygous missense variants cause Baker-Gordon syndrome, a condition characterized by intellectual disability, hyperkinetic movement disorder, sleep disturbances, behavioral abnormalities, and ophthalmic anomalies — features that substantially overlap with ASD symptomatology. The evidence base is strong across clinical, functional, and model organism domains, with a clearly defined dominant-negative pathogenic mechanism. While direct evidence specifically linking SYT1 to a formal ASD diagnosis remains preliminary, the neurodevelopmental phenotype, brain expression pattern, and emerging genetic data from ASD cohorts support SYT1 as a credible ASD-associated gene warranting systematic evaluation in large ASD sequencing studies.
